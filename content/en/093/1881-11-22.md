# Tuesday, 22 November 1881

I cried so much yesterday that my aunt came to ask whether something hurt, a touching concern to which I replied that I had a sore nose.
It is impossible to imagine how despairing this exile to the Midi is for me. It seems as though everything is going to be over. I, who had come back intoxicated with the idea of staying quietly and working — working hard, without respite, keeping up with the movement… And now everything collapses again! And while others keep moving forward in the midst of artistic Paris, I shall be there doing nothing, or chasing after an outdoor painting, which is a frightfully difficult thing… Look at Breslau[^1] — it is not her peasant woman that won her anything.
In a word, my heart cracks and breaks at all this. This evening I saw Charcot,[^2] who says the illness has not worsened since last year; as for what I have had these past eight days, it is a chill [words blacked out: of no consequence] and will go away very quickly. As for the Midi, it is the same story — one must go, or else shut oneself up absolutely like a prisoner, otherwise there is a constant risk of catching something serious, since the right lung is affected — and yet, it seems, I am fortunate: it is a curable illness that has localized and is not increasing, despite all my alleged imprudences. He told me the same thing last winter and I simply refused to listen; now I hesitate, and from four o’clock on I sit weeping, as I did yesterday, at this idea of leaving Paris again, of interrupting myself…
It is true that if I am often as I have been these past days, Paris will do me little good… And that is what drives me to despair. To surrender. To admit defeat. To say, yes, the doctors are right. Yes, I am ill.
Ah! No — everything is decidedly going badly.

[^2]: Jean-Martin Charcot (1825–1893), professor at the Salpêtrière hospital and the most celebrated French physician of his era, renowned for his work on neurological diseases. He also treated pulmonary patients. His confirmation that Marie's right lung is affected is the clearest diagnosis yet of the tuberculosis that would kill her in 1884, three years later.

[^1]: Louise Breslau (1856–1927), Swiss-German painter, Marie's most formidable rival at the Académie Julian. She had recently won an Honorable Mention at the Salon (1879) for her painting of a young woman reading; Marie's reference to "her peasant woman" alludes to a later outdoor or genre subject. Breslau went on to become one of the most successful female painters of the Third Republic.
