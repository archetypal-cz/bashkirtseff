# Tuesday 7 September 1875
%% Quelle position, bon Dieu, quelle position ! Nous devons partir pour nous arrêter à Marseille et, là, engager mes diamants. Attendre l'argent ici est impossible, nous manquerions les fêtes de Florence. %%

What a situation, good Lord, what a situation! We must leave, stopping at Marseille to pawn my diamonds there. Waiting for the money here is impossible; we would miss the festivities in Florence.

%% 041.0218 %%
%% Il n'y a rien au monde de plus affreux que de manquer d'argent. Ah ! ceux qui ont éprouvé me comprennent. C'est le comble de l'abaissement de l'intelligence humaine, on devient l'égale d'une table, d'une bûche, d'une pierre. On est garrotté, misérable, pitoyable. Ah ! bigre de bigre ! %%
%% 2026-09-29T01:28:41 RSR: Restored from tome05.docx ¶4815 (missing from original extraction, 2026-09-28 rebuild). %%
There is nothing in the world more dreadful than being short of money. Ah, those who have known it will understand me. It is the utmost degradation of human intelligence; one becomes the equal of a table, a log, a stone. One is bound hand and foot, wretched, pitiful. Ah! *Bigre de bigre!*

%% Nous rentrons du Gymnase, on donnait "Frou-Frou", comédie qui laisse à désirer, mais bien jouée, et il y a de très belles scènes, mais je n'admire pas le tout comme dans "Le Sphynx". "Frou-Frou", l'enfant gâtée de la maison épouse de Lertoris. Le monsieur est aimé de Louise, sœur de sa femme. Un beau jour il vient à Gilberte, la femme, l'idée de prendre sa sœur chez elle. Au bout de deux mois celle-ci, sans mauvaise intention aucune, se fait aimer de son neveu, enfant de sept ans et de son beau- frère, pas criminellement, Dieu me garde de dire cela. Le quatrième acte les trouve à Venise, Gilberte et son bon-ami, comme disent les Niçois, survient le mari, se bat avec l'amant, le tue et ayant fait cette belle action s'en va tranquillement chez lui. Je comprends cela autrement que vous, la femme ne meurt pas à cause des remords, elle meurt parce qu'on lui a tué son amant. Seulement je trouve les caractères du mari et de la sœur bien mal exposés. %% 2026-09-29T01:28:41 RSR: Restored from tome05.docx ¶4819–4821 (missing from original extraction, 2026-09-28 rebuild). %%
We have just come back from the Gymnase; they were giving "Frou-Frou", a comedy that leaves something to be desired but is well acted, and there are some very fine scenes, though I do not admire the whole as I did with "Le Sphinx". "Frou-Frou", the spoiled child of the house, marries de Lertoris. The gentleman is loved by Louise, his wife's sister. One fine day Gilberte, the wife, takes it into her head to have her sister come and live with her. After two months the sister, without the least ill intention, has won the love of her nephew, a child of seven, and of her brother-in-law — not criminally, God forbid I should say that. Now, de Valréas, a young man who had asked for her hand, Gilberte's hand, loves her still, and about this time so strongly that he declares himself in earnest; she orders him to go away, though she loves him, but after a scene with her sister — exasperated, wounded, jealous — she goes off with him. The fourth act finds them in Venice, Gilberte and her *bon-ami*, as they say in Nice; the husband turns up, fights the lover, kills him, and having done this fine deed goes quietly home. In the last act the dying wife comes to his house, after a year of nursing [sic] the poor, of sacrifices and a heap of such things, and the cad of a husband forgives her on her deathbed. And she wants him to marry her sister, which the wretch will not fail to do, I am sure of it. I understand it differently from you: the woman does not die of remorse, she dies because they have killed her lover. Only I find the characters of the husband and the sister very poorly drawn. Louise is virtuous, but quite innocently she has brought about all these fine goings-on, and the husband, while loving Frou-Frou, grows attached to her. Poor Frou-Frou! You are very silly, my dear. When you saw that rogue of a husband treat you as a child or as a mistress, as you put it, you should have taken your revenge — though for my part I would have been content: your husband treated you adorably. But you were jealous of your sister; your sister looked after your son, she ran the house, your husband turned to her for everything serious; you were piqued, poor silly thing. But after all, since you were jealous, and above all since you loved Valréas, well then, you should have taken him in at home, secretly, and not made this absurd scandal by running off to Venice!

That does not show in my analysis, but it will show presently. In a word, my mind is troubled; I long, I long to love someone; I imagine I am Gilberte — well, you can guess what I mean, to put it as gently as possible. I am horribly vexed at my defeat; I remember everything from the beginning, this winter, and this spring above all. I am irritated and grieved — no, angry, furious. I would gladly give him a thrashing with a stick, and punches (now I no longer know how to spell that word) in the face; I would like to drench him with cold water, send him rolling in the mud, tie him up, beat him again and then throw him into a pit full of filth.

%% 041.0227 %%
%% Voilà comme on se venge ! Oh ! jamais je n'étais si vexée, si humiliée. Moi qui pensais à la miséricorde, à la clémence, moi je suis humiliée, dédaignée ! Le mot est dur. Mais sacrebleu ! That is how one takes revenge! Oh, never have I been so vexed, so humiliated. I, who was thinking of mercy, of clemency — I am humiliated, disdained! The word is harsh. But *sacrebleu*! It is true. That will teach me to imagine myself a divinity who has only to make a sign, only to form a wish, to be obeyed.

%% 041.0228 %%
%% Et ma tante, qui en voyant cette créature ignoble assise au café, sur le boulevard, commence à dire des choses qui piquent mon amour-propre. %% 2026-09-29T01:28:41 RSR: Restored from tome05.docx ¶4827–4832 (missing from original extraction, 2026-09-28 rebuild). %%
And my aunt, who, on seeing this ignoble creature sitting at the café on the boulevard, starts saying things that sting my pride. I had stopped thinking of this man as a personal matter, and thought of him as of an affair like the one with the Howards. In an instant he ceased to be Girofla — no, not in an instant, but little by little since we left Nice — he became a gentleman who was dropping *us*, *us*, mark you, not me. I no longer thought of him as a man who had disdained me, but as a gentleman who was gradually ceasing to know us, who was turning away like so many others. And that was such a consolation, a joy even. But now they awaken other ideas in me; they strike the woman's sensitive chord, the woman's pride. In no other woman is it as developed as in me. I have ambition in everything, and
Be it vice or virtue,
My heart, unburdened,
Is willing to be laid low.

%% 041.0237 %%
