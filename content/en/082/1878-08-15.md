I have done what I intended: I met Schwarz and accosted him. "Monsieur," said I, "yesterday a disagreeable misunderstanding occurred; M. Dourassoff wished to introduce you, thinking it for the best; you did not wish it. And I have come to tell you that had M. Dourassoff consulted me, I would not have wished it either. We make acquaintances among Russians — but with foreigners it is different. I have not the slightest desire to make your acquaintance. And when you next address a lady, I advise you to remove your hat."

Dourassoff came to repeat this to me. In short, it is quite simply a scandal. What an idea, too, to imagine a narrow-minded German to be an intelligent Frenchman. I am so thoroughly unhinged by all of this that I tremble with rage and shame... didn't I tell you that My God would come to my aid to administer some correction or other...

> Soden
> *15 August 1878*
> *Dear Count,*
> *I begin by telling you that I am at Soden with my aunt, Maman being kept in Paris by the grave state of my grandfather's health.*
> *Could you tell me what sort of person a Prince Auguste de Hohenhole-Oehringen is? Here, moreover, is what it is about. One of our friends made his acquaintance, and recently, when our whole party — which is quite numerous and charming — was gathered together, our friend wished to introduce M. de Hohenhole to us, and he said, loudly enough to be heard by all of us: No, thank you.*
> *I confess that this shocked me utterly and, taking the part of all our ladies, I was foolish enough to tell him that, had I been consulted, it is I who would not have wished to make his acquaintance. Perhaps I was wrong, but you must admit too that the gentleman was rather rude, all the more so as the next day he went and said that I had said impertinent things to him without knowing him and without*
> *reason (which makes me pass for a madwoman), and that he would take his revenge. An old princess from Petersburg, whom you know well, said he looked stupid, not knowing that he understood Russian; and I drew his caricature, but frankly those are not insults grave enough to make anyone as furious as he became. One must suppose either that he is mad or that there is a third person involved. I am receiving extraordinary letters which, though unsigned, seem very clearly to be written by him. Who knows whether some imbecile is not amusing himself by writing letters to him as if they came from me or from someone in our party? In any case I am sorry to have got mixed up in all this, the more so as my remark will not correct the gentleman if he is ill-bred or narrow by nature.*
> *I do not know where my letter will find you, but knowing that you are still our great friend, I thought of you for information about this gentleman, who goes about everywhere speaking ill of me because we made a little fun of him; and he is very ill, and consequently very spiteful.*
> *Answer me quickly and send us your news at Soden, Kurhaus. Au revoir.*
> *My sincere regards.*
> Marie Bashkirtseff
