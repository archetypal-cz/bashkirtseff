## Sunday, 11 December 1876

%% 067.1267 %%

I went to church. The weather is so fine that I went for a walk after trying to skate while there was still no one there. On our way out we ran into Princess Souvoroff, who was walking towards us with d'Aspremont.

Later, when we were back at the Skating-rink, this celebrated hero was working wonders there with his aides-de-camp.

%% 067.1271 %%

Maman spoke with Lady Cawper's husband, whom she had met in Rome; my aunt and Dina were surrounded... by General Bihovetz; and I strolled round them with little Olga, both of us eating galettes, showing myself to Princess Kotchoubey, sister-in-law of the prince I saw in Russia. Her husband recognised Maman and greeted her this morning at church — he knew her when she was as tall as I am.

Meanwhile Girofla glided over to my aunt.

%% 067.1273 %%

I went up and complimented him on his skating, with a rather disdainful air. Then I walked away without waiting for him to finish thanking me.

You understand — he is an ill-mannered cad, so I am patronising and disdainful.

%% 067.1275 %%

As for Belle-de-jour, I do not even look at him.

%% 067.1276 %%

It is time, it seems to me, to become independent and to stop smiling at people who despise you.

%% 2026-02-10T12:30:00 LAN: "torsades" = twisted coils of hair. "chemisette de batiste" = fine linen blouse. "foulard" = silk scarf fabric. "figure" = face (period, NOT body). "cachet" = style/distinction. %%
%% On a vu Pelikan, les Anitchkoff, tous ces gens qu'on voit toujours. Et le soir je suis allée à l'Opéra. Les cheveux plats devant et retombant en torsades à peine roulées sur la nuque, revenait s'attacher par un paquet de boucles devant. Cette tête échevelée plate et grosse est une innovation et me donne l'air innocente, c'est assorti à une sorte de costume russe, chemisette de batiste sous une robe de foulard... la même qu'à l'Opéra de Paris. Des perles au cou, pas de gants. Je ne me ressemblais pas, j'étais un vrai tableau. Je ne fais pas l'éloge de ma figure, je laisse cela aux autres, mais je rend justice à l'ensemble du genre, en cachet. %%
We saw Pelikan, the Anitchkoffs, all the people one always sees. And in the evening I went to the Opera. Hair flat in front, falling behind in barely rolled coils on the nape, and brought round again to be fastened with a cluster of curls in front. This flat, full, tousled head is an innovation and gives me an innocent air; it goes with a sort of Russian costume, a fine linen chemisette under a foulard dress... the same one as at the Paris Opera. Pearls at the neck, no gloves. I did not look like myself; I was a real picture. I do not praise my face — I leave that to others — but I do justice to the style of the whole, to its distinction.

%% 067.1278 %%
%% [#Kernberger](../../_original/_glossary/culture/literature/KATHERINE_KERNBERGER.md) %%
%% La Vigier est en face avec lady Cawper et cette dernière lui raconte un tas de choses tout en me lorgnant. Girofla n'apparait que vers le troisième acte, et me lorgne ainsi que ses acolytes. J'étais assez remarquable pour mériter cet honneur. Personne ne s'arrange avec autant d'originalité, de simplicité et de bon goût, que moi, surtout de bon goût. %%
La Vigier is opposite with Lady Cawper, who tells her a quantity of things while eyeing me through her lorgnette. Girofla does not appear until the third act and eyes me likewise, as do his acolytes. I was remarkable enough to deserve the honour. No one dresses with as much originality, simplicity, and good taste as I — above all good taste.

Mme Anitchkoff leaves well before the end; I pretend to go out and hide, closing the door again, to see what le Surprenant will do — [words blacked out: he only comes near my family when I am not there.]

%% 2026-02-10T12:30:00 LAN: "devergonde" - debauched, dissolute. "les cocottes" - kept women, courtesans. %% Mais il m'arrive une chose assez curieuse. Je commence à admirer Gautier à force de regarder ses sœurs. Elles étaient à côté de nous ce soir et, pendant que je parlais à la demoiselle, je vis entrer chez eux leur frère, mais pas celui que je connais, un autre, l'aîné, Michel ? Plus petit et moins beau qu'Albert, mais dépravé comme personne ! Cet air dévergondé, me charme tout en me déplaisant. Ce soir il était superbe. Un teint mat, les yeux les yeux de famille, des cheveux propres et jolis... et toute la tenue charmante. Il m'a remis dans l'esprit Larderei et Antonelli... c'est tout la même série. Il passe sa vie avec les cocottes[^8] ce qui lui donne cet air dévergondé et intéressant qui me dégoûte et me charme... ou plutôt m'agace et me donne des idées folles. %%
But something rather curious is happening to me. I am beginning to admire Gautier from looking so much at his sisters. They were next to us this evening, and while I was talking to the young lady I saw their brother come into their box — not the one I know, but another, the elder — Michel? Shorter and less handsome than Albert, but as depraved as they come! That debauched air charms me even as it displeases me. This evening he was superb. A matt complexion, the eyes, the family eyes, neat pretty hair... and his whole bearing charming. He brought Larderel and Antonelli back to my mind... they are all of the same breed. He spends his life with the *cocottes*,[^8] which gives him that debauched and interesting air that disgusts and charms me... or rather irritates me and gives me wild ideas.

[^8]: *Cocottes* — courtesans, kept women of the demi-monde.

Since I need to speak of someone, I speak of Michel Gautier — and people laugh at me.

I detest myself and I detest my writing this evening.

%% > J’ai déjà dessiné ici un Antonelli plutôt intime que de grande cérémonie. Je l’ai portraicturé tel que je l’ai vu de bien près. Le grand homme d’Etat n’est plus. L’illustre mort est entré dans l’immortalité rayonnante. La statue qui était dans le rocher de Carrare va sortir du bloc et prendre une forme définie. C’est impossible que Figaro, qui était lu par le grand cardinal — j’en ai eu des preuves certaines — ne jette pas sur cette tombe cette couronne de fleurs sans épines, que d’ordinaire il n’aime point donner aux vivants. Aussi bien cette gloire qui vient de s’allumer au feu qui dure toujours, a-t-elle éclairé d’une lumière parfois violente, mais toujours pure, le clair-obscur de ce siècle. Il me semble que cette âme superbe qui vient de partir est comme une lampe qu’on emporte d’un salon — la clarté est diminuée. Quand Pie partira à son tour, le monde idéal sera dans l’obscurité. Donc, disons tout d’abord que ce qui caractérise le cardinal Antonelli, c’est qu’il fut de son temps. Cela ne semblera un paradoxe qu’aux regards superficiels. Antonelli a eu le goût de l’esprit moderne avant de le combattre. Il en eut le sens même en le combattant. Ce caractère qui ne se brisait point parce qu’il savait plier, appartient au dix-neuvième siècle. Il est bon de le retenir contre ceux qui voudront l’accaparer au profit du moyen âge. Il a pris à notre époque ses armes et son langage. Après avoir partagé toutes les hautes passions de ce temps, Antonelli en a partagé toutes les dernières et logiques désillusions. Or, il faut être de son temps pour mériter de lui survivre. Enfin, ce ministre a eu dans son département les affaires du monde entier pendant plus de trente ans. Son nom et sa figure resteront tant qu’il y aura une histoire de l’humanité au dix-neuvième siècle. %%

> CARDINAL ANTONELLI
> I have already sketched here an Antonelli more intimate than ceremonial. I portrayed him as I saw him at very close quarters. The great statesman is no more. The illustrious dead man has entered into radiant immortality. The statue that lay within the Carrara rock is about to emerge from the block and take on a definite form. It is impossible that *Figaro*, which was read by the great cardinal — I have had certain proof of it — should not lay upon this tomb that wreath of thornless flowers which it does not, as a rule, care to bestow upon the living. And indeed this glory, which has just been kindled at the fire that burns for ever, lit with a light sometimes violent but always pure the chiaroscuro of this century. It seems to me that this proud soul which has just departed is like a lamp carried out of a drawing room — the brightness is diminished. When Pius departs in his turn, the ideal world will be in darkness. Let us say, then, first of all, that what characterises Cardinal Antonelli is that he belonged to his time. This will seem a paradox only to superficial eyes. Antonelli had a taste for the modern spirit before he fought it. He had a feeling for it even while fighting it. That character, which never broke because it knew how to bend, belongs to the nineteenth century. It is well to remember this against those who will seek to annex him for the Middle Ages. He took his weapons and his language from our age. Having shared all the lofty passions of this time, Antonelli shared all its final and logical disillusionments. Now, one must belong to one's time to deserve to outlive it. Lastly, this minister had in his department the affairs of the entire world for more than thirty years. His name and his countenance will endure as long as there is a history of mankind in the nineteenth century.

> In 1846 Antonelli was among those who sought to baptise Liberty according to the Catholic rite — Liberty, which may perhaps soon die a freethinker and be given a civil burial. 1848 came. It seemed as though all the déclassés of Italy had staked on that number and won a quaterne in the lottery. It was a veritable Bacchanal. Reality shattered Antonelli's fine dream. > To the new right, born of the formula "Equality, Liberty, Fraternity," it resolutely opposed the old right. History alone will be able to say whether Antonelli lost his illusions too soon. But it will grant him a superior gift of foresight. There was something of the prophet in him. And Antonelli brought to this new role the three essential qualities of the statesman: — perseverance, — knowledge, — courage. When people said to him: "Eminence, the social and anti-religious revolution is crushed; the man of the nineteenth century will not return to the excesses of 1793, which disgust him!" Antonelli would answer: "I tell you that man, like the dog in the Book, will return to his vomit!" And he did not lie.

> The role Antonelli played was not of his choosing. He loved "sub lege libertas." But he had understood the great contemporary truth which our fathers had not even glimpsed; I will explain. Christ cast all truths into the universe, as his Father had cast all the stars into it. Just as the light of some of those stars has taken more than three thousand years to reach the earth, so the truth of which I speak remained in shadow for nearly nineteen centuries. This truth is: — Democracy can do man nothing but harm, and every liberty that comes from below destroys nations. So Antonelli, measuring his resistance to the vigour of the attack, and exaggerating, as all initiators do, the truth they teach, condemned all democracy and all liberty. I am one of those who love the second, if they do not love the first. I am therefore quite at ease in explaining Antonelli's attitude. He was, however, under no illusion. He did not hide from his intimates that the temporal papacy would be broken. When it was, he admitted that he would not see it reborn in his lifetime. He did not have the ecstasy of Ezekiel, who in the Babylonian captivity saw the Temple rebuilt in a dream. Events were able to overthrow Antonelli; they never surprised or astonished him. Defeat was part of his system. To measure and understand the whole of the cardinal's system one would have to live a hundred years — for this system takes more account of the future than of the present — one would need to have one's feet in this century and one's head in the next. The God in whom Antonelli believed did not guarantee him Today, but Tomorrow!

C’est vrai de dire que Pie IX lui avait confié un royaume, et qu’Antonelli, en mourant, n’a remis à Pie IX qu’une épave. Mais cette épave indique l’écueil. Ce système fut-il bon ? Un système de concessions et d’atermoiements eût-il été préférable ? Je ne le crois pas. Mais à coup sûr, la papauté spirituelle dont Antonelli a été le premier serviteur, n’est pas diminuée. Un seul homme a pu prendre en 1870 la défense de la France contre la Prusse, et ce fut Pie IX. Son cabinet était encore, à la veille de sa mort, comme l’ancien milliaire doré d’où partaient dans l’univers toutes les voies romaines. La Révolution a pu prendre les murs de Rome, sans en avoir les âmes — de même on peut conquérir des montagnes sans en avoir les aigles ! Antonelli a été plus fort que la Force. Sans lui, personne n’aurait osé protester contre la politique du fait et du profit. Il est le spirituel contre le matériel. Il est la pensée contre le corps. Il est enfin la protestation la plus éclatante contre ces deux puissances modernes, le canon et l’argent. Alors que la voix du pape semble descendre du ciel, la sienne semble monter de nos abîmes. > Antonelli sought, not to kill the Revolution — he could not — but to render it sterile. Above all he sought to extricate the papacy, for a moment compromised, from contemporary adventures. It is true to say that Pius IX had entrusted a kingdom to him, and that Antonelli, in dying, has handed back to Pius IX nothing but a wreck. But that wreck marks the reef. Was this system a good one? Would a system of concessions and temporising have been preferable? I do not think so. But assuredly the spiritual papacy, of which Antonelli was the first servant, is not diminished. One man alone could take up the defence of France against Prussia in 1870, and that man was Pius IX. His cabinet was still, on the eve of his death, like the golden milestone of old from which all the Roman roads set out across the world. The Revolution was able to take the walls of Rome without winning its souls — just as one may conquer mountains without winning their eagles! Antonelli was stronger than Force. Without him no one would have dared to protest against the politics of the accomplished fact and of profit. He is the spiritual against the material. He is thought against the body. He is, in short, the most resounding protest against those two modern powers, the cannon and money. While the Pope's voice seems to come down from heaven, his seems to rise up from our depths. It is the better understood. Instead of clearing the tree of Liberty of its caterpillars, he preferred to uproot it. That is a little brutal; but it is so much in human nature to take revenge on things for the faults of peoples. And Antonelli was a man. Pius is a saint. Antonelli believed in miracles, but he did not count on them. He looked towards heaven, but also at his feet. His known work is immense. But greater still is his unknown work, the work that will begin tomorrow upon his tomb. It is a book whose pages only the future will turn. On its success Antonelli has staked his life and his glory. Let us hope he wins. But how sorely he will be missed, first of all, by the great conservative party, by civil society as much as by religious society! We shall soon see, when the cardinal lies in his tomb, what an immense abyss his red cassock concealed!

> What I said there is true. The great cardinal's personality was somewhat absorbed by the light that surrounds Pius IX like a halo. His silhouette will take on more defined outlines as he leaves the Pope to enter the tomb. His statue can never be made too high. Every man in whom there still remain the instinct for the ideal and a disgust for certain triumphs of brute force will subscribe to that statue. I do not care to treat certain questions of religion, and I have been able to avoid them in this second portrait of Antonelli. Nevertheless I will say that in calling the Catholic religion to battle he necessarily gave it a manly energy which it perhaps lacked in Italy. It seemed to be growing feeble under the homage of a passionate crowd, as the toe of the St Peter in the basilica wears away under the kisses of the faithful. As regards the domain of speculative politics, Antonelli's influence was enormous. He will have been the genius of the conservative party's resistance. He will have been the most energetic defender of the principle of authority. Of all this noise that is made two metres above the eternal silence, there will at least remain to the great cardinal the murmur of admiration of souls of every religion who protest against materialist policies, by whatever names they are adorned. First servant of the papacy, he could say that he had always been "standing before men or on his knees before God." Indeed, his life was like the churches of Rome, where there is nothing to rest on and one must kneel or stand. He kept the oath he had sworn — he never left his master, and, a voluntary prisoner, he never came out of his prison. Dead, he will not come out of it. His body will pass straight from his bed in the Vatican to the vaults of St Peter's in Rome, the chapel of the papacy.
