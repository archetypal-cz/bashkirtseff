# Thursday, 28 December 1882

%% Il s'agit bien de cela ! Je suis poitrinaire.[^1] Il me l'a dit aujourd'hui, soignez-vous, il faut tenter de guérir, vous le regretterez. C'est un jeune homme et qui a l'air intelligent mon docteur; à mes objections contre les vésicatoires et autres infamies il répond que je le regretterai et qu'il n'a jamais de sa vie vu une malade aussi extraordinaire. Et aussi qu'à mon aspect on ne me donnerait jamais, jamais ma maladie, j'ai l'air florissant en effet et les deux poumons sont atteints, le gauche beaucoup moins pourtant. La première fois que j'ai eu mal à gauche ça a été en sortant des saintes catacombes à Kieff[^2] où nous avons tous été demander au Bon Dieu et aux reliques de ses saints de me guérir à grand renfort de messes et de roubles. Il y a huit jours on n'entendait encore presque rien au poumon gauche. Il m'a demandé si j'ai des parents poitrinaires. Oui, le père de grand-papa et ses deux sœurs la comtesse de Toulouse-Lautrec[^3] et Mme Stralbo. Un arrière grand-père et deux grandes tantes. %%

Never mind all that! I am consumptive.[^1] He told me so today: "Take care of yourself — one must attempt to be cured; you will regret it." My doctor is a young man with an intelligent air; to my objections against blister plasters and other outrages he replies that I shall regret it, and that he has never in his life seen so extraordinary a patient. And also that from my appearance one would never, never guess my illness — I look flourishing indeed, and yet both lungs are affected, though the left considerably less. The first time I felt pain on the left was on coming out of the holy catacombs at Kiev,[^2] where we had all gone to beg God and the relics of His saints to cure me — at great expense in masses and roubles. A week ago hardly anything could be heard in the left lung yet. He asked whether I have consumptive relatives. Yes: my great-grandfather and his two sisters — the Comtesse de Toulouse-Lautrec[^3] and Mme Stralbo. One great-great-grandfather and two great-aunts.

%% 2026-10-02T15:31:37 RSR: Accents restored (OCR-stripped in the extraction; 7 words) per tome15.docx ¶1603–1606, Mon Journal t.15. %%

"In any case, whatever one may say — you are consumptive." I was swaying slightly on my legs going down the staircase of this good man who takes such interest in so original a patient. It can be arrested if I do what is necessary. That is to say: blister plasters and the south of France. To disfigure my shoulders for a year and go into exile.

%% 097.0299 %%

What is a year set against one's whole life? Not that my life is beautiful, by the way.

%% Je suis très calme mais un peu étonnée d'être seule dans le secret de mes malheurs. Et les tireuses de cartes qui me prédisent tant de bonheurs, pourtant la Jacob m'a annoncé une maladie. La voilà. Pour que sa prédiction soit entièrement réalisée il manque: le grand succès, l'argent, le mariage et puis l'amour d'un homme marié. Enfin, ça me fait mal du côté gauche, le moins malade. Potain[^4] n'a jamais voulu dire que les poumons étaient atteints, il employait les formules ordinaires en pareil cas, les bronches, la bronchite etc. Il vaut mieux savoir au juste, ça me décide à tout faire sauf le départ cette année. %%

I am very calm, but a little astonished to be alone in the secret of my misfortunes. And the card-readers who predict so much happiness for me — though la Jacob did announce an illness. Here it is. For her prediction to be entirely realised, the following are still missing: great success, money, marriage, and then the love of a married man. In any case, I feel pain on the left — the less ill side. Potain[^4] always refused to say the lungs were affected; he used the usual formulas in such cases — the bronchi, bronchitis, etc. It is better to know exactly; it decides me to do everything, except leave this year.

Next winter I shall have the painting of the Holy Women[^5] as a reason to explain the journey south. This winter would mean starting all the irritations of last year over again. Everything — but not the south, and trust in God.

%% Enfin ce qui fait qu'il a tant dit ce docteur c'est que depuis qu'il me voit ça va plus mal. Il soigne mes oreilles et je ne lui ai parlé de la poitrine que par hasard en riant et alors il a écouté et il m'a ordonné des granulés (il y a un mois) et surtout des saletés sur la peau, ce à quoi je n'ai pas pu me décider espérant que ça n'avancerait pas si vite. %%

In any case the reason the doctor has said all this is that since he has been treating me things have been going worse. He is treating my ears, and I only mentioned my chest by chance, laughing about it — and then he listened, ordered granules (a month ago), and above all those things put on the skin, which I could not bring myself to accept, hoping the illness would not progress so fast.

So — I am consumptive? For only two or three years. And in sum it is not advanced enough for me to die of it; it is only very tiresome.

Oh, yes. But how to explain my healthy appearance — and that I can no longer get into bodices that were made well before I was ill and when nothing was in question. One must assume I shall grow thin suddenly; it may be because I am young, and because of my broad shoulders, rounded bosom, and Spanish hips. I cannot get over all these catastrophes.

In short — let them give me ten years more, and during those ten years, glory and love, and I shall die content at thirty. No — at thirty-five. If there were someone with whom to negotiate, I would make a bargain.

Carolus is older than that and one could still love him, especially if one had loved him in advance. Ah, wretchedness. But I should like to recover... I shall put on as many blister plasters as one likes — but I want to paint.

Ah! I told you I would have to die. God, unable to give me what would make life possible, gets out of it by killing me. After overwhelming me with miseries, He kills me to put an end to it. I told you I would have to die — it could not last, this thirst for everything, these colossal aspirations; it could not last. I told you, long ago, years ago, in Nice, when I still vaguely glimpsed all that I would need in order to live. But others have more and do not die! Come now!

At this he asks what I think of Heaven — I had said that Heaven was mistreating me. What do I think of it? Not much good. He believes I believe [crossed out: that there is nothing and] that there is something all the same. Yes — it is possible. I read him Musset's "L'espoir en Dieu," and he replies with the invocation or the imprecations of Frank...[^6] "I want to live!!" So do I. But look — I find this position of one condemned, or nearly so, rather amusing. It is a pose, an emotion; I contain a mystery; death has touched me with its finger; there is a certain charm in that — it is new, to begin with.

And to be able *for real* to speak of my death — it is interesting, and I repeat: it amuses me. It is a pity one cannot without inconvenience have any other audience than my confessor Julian.

[^1]: Consumptive (*poitrinaire*): the 19th-century term for tuberculosis of the lungs. The actual word *tuberculose* was not yet in common use; *poitrine* (chest) gave the noun *poitrinaire*. The diagnosis here — given on 28 December 1882 — is confirmed by two physicians. Marie would die of tuberculosis on 31 October 1884, twenty-two months later.
[^2]: The holy catacombs at Kiev: the famous Kiev Pechersk Lavra (Monastery of the Caves), one of the most important Orthodox pilgrimage sites in Eastern Europe. The Bashkirtseff family clearly stopped there on their autumn journey and prayed for Marie's health — an act of piety that did not prevent the diagnosis.
[^3]: Comtesse de Toulouse-Lautrec: Marie's family had a collateral connection with the Toulouse-Lautrec family. The Comte Alphonse de Toulouse-Lautrec, father of the painter Henri, was Marie's distant relative. The family had a strong hereditary predisposition to tuberculosis.
[^4]: Potain: Dr. Pierre-Carl Potain (1825–1901), one of the most eminent Parisian physicians of the century, a specialist in cardiovascular and pulmonary diseases who taught at the Paris medical faculty. His use of vague formulae (*bronchitis*, etc.) was a common medical practice when telling patients the truth about tuberculosis was considered psychologically harmful.
[^5]: The Holy Women (*les Saintes femmes*): a large religious painting Marie had in mind — the scene at the tomb on Easter morning. She worked on preparatory studies for it but it was never completed.
[^6]: Frank: the hero of Musset's dramatic poem *La Coupe et les Lèvres* (1832). Frank's passionate invocation — "I want to live!" — is one of Musset's most celebrated lyric outbursts. Julian citing it in response to Marie's reading of "L'espoir en Dieu" turns their evening into an impromptu literary wake.
