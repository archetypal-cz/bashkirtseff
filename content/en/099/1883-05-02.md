# Wednesday, 2 May 1883

%% 099.0323 %%
%% [#Kernberger](../../_original/_glossary/culture/literature/KATHERINE_KERNBERGER.md) %%
%% [#Mortality](../../_original/_glossary/culture/themes/MORTALITY.md) %%
%% 2026-03-10T10:00:00 TR: "nature morte" — still life (painting). %%
%% Je travaille au portrait de Bojidar et Mlle Canrobert a une nature morte. %%
I am working on Bojidar's portrait and Mlle Canrobert is doing a still life.

%% 099.0324 %%
%% [#Kernberger](../../_original/_glossary/culture/literature/KATHERINE_KERNBERGER.md) %%
%% 2026-03-10T10:00:00 TR: SPELLING: "pantomine" — Marie's spelling for "pantomime" (corrected in translation). "se brosse" — paints roughly, dashes off. "pancartes" — placards, signs (for the performance). "Pierrot peintre" — Pierrot the Painter (commedia dell'arte). "gambades" — capers, frolics. "se foule" — sprains. %%
%% Et nous sommes surtout très occupés de notre pantomine; Bojidar a composé un scénario assez drôle et nous trouvons tous les jours quelque chose. Il se brosse des toiles et des pancartes. Ça s'appelle Pierrot peintre. Et voilà que cet idiot de Bojidar descend au salon et en faisant des gambades se foule le genou. %%
And we are above all very busy with our pantomime: Bojidar has composed a rather funny scenario and every day we find something new. He dashes off canvases and placards. It is called *Pierrot the Painter*. And then that idiot Bojidar goes downstairs to the drawing room and sprains his knee while cavorting about.

%% On envoie chercher le médecin et en voilà pour quinze jours, c'est désolant. C'était si joli. Mme Cassandre le prend pour elle et se pâme. %%
The doctor is sent for — and there goes a fortnight: it is dreadful. It was so delightful. The scene represents Pierrot's studio. Pierrot (Bojidar) is in love with Columbine. Mme Cassandre (Émile) arrives — thinking she is going to her milliner, for Pierrot has changed the signs. He entreats Mme Cassandre to let him paint her portrait; she consents. Columbine (myself) stands behind her while Pierrot blows kisses to Columbine as he paints. Mme Cassandre takes the kisses for herself and swoons.

%% Enfin elle s'endort et Pierrot fait une déclaration à Colombine et la demande en mariage. De quoi vivrons-nous demande Colombine. Alors Pierrot lui montre ses peintures. Des parodies déjà faites cet après-midi, des principaux succès du Salon, tels que l'Amour au village de Bastien. Ici se placent des actualités et des farces. %%
She falls asleep at last, and Pierrot declares his love to Columbine and proposes. *What shall we live on?* asks Columbine. Then Pierrot shows her his paintings — parodies already done this afternoon of the chief successes of the Salon, such as Bastien's *Amour au village*. Here various topical jokes and farces are inserted.

%% Colombine trouve que cette peinture est atroce et Pierrot désespéré s'assoit sur Mme Cassandre qui se lève en sursaut et Pierrot va rouler par terre, puis il lui explique que c'était pour prendre une mesure. Mme Cassandre trouve son portrait si affreux qu'elle tombe et meure. Colombine est désespérée et Pierrot crève ses toiles cause de tout. Mme Cassandre réssuscite. Pierrot lui jure qu'il ne peindra plus jamais, il montre une pancarte où est peinte la fameuse colonnade avec une inscription énorme : Bourse, il fait le geste de faire des porte-monnaies. Mme Cassandre ravie lui donne Colombine en mariage. %%
Columbine finds the painting atrocious, and the despairing Pierrot sits on Mme Cassandre, who leaps up — and Pierrot goes rolling to the floor; then he explains it was to take a measurement. Mme Cassandre finds her portrait so frightful that she collapses and dies. Columbine is in despair, and Pierrot slashes his canvases, the cause of it all. Mme Cassandre revives. Pierrot swears never to paint again — he shows a placard on which the famous colonnade is painted with an enormous inscription: *BOURSE* — and mimes the gesture of making purses. Mme Cassandre, delighted, gives him Columbine in marriage.

%% Je devais aller à l'Opéra; mais à quoi bon ? C'est-à-dire. J'ai pensé un instant y aller pour qu'en m'y montrant jolie cela vienne aux oreilles de Jules. Et pourquoir faire ? Je ne sais pas. Enfin est-ce bête ! Est-ce fou, que je plaise à des gens à qui je ne tiens pas du tout et qu'en revanche... Oh ! c'est sûr que je ne lui plairai pas. Et puis cette Mackay le tient et puis il n'a pas le temps... Mais ce serait si gentil, si charmant, si bien ! Je serais si contente ! %%
I was to go to the Opéra — but what is the use? That is, I thought for a moment of going so that, by showing myself there looking well, word might reach Jules. And why do it? I do not know. In short — how foolish! How mad — that I please people I care nothing about, while in return… Oh, it is certain he will not like me. And then that Mackay woman has a hold on him, and then he has no time… But it would be so pleasant, so charming, so fine! I should be so happy!

One must be careful, all the more so since it would really be for nothing at all — for after all I have no serious designs on this great artist. Would I marry him? No. Well, then?

Well, then? And then… one doesn't know… Yet… However… In sum… Well, why must I always dig down to heaven knows where? I have a mad desire to please this great man, and there it is. And Saint-Marceaux too. Which of them more? Either. One of them would be enough for me. It is an interest… in life. My face is changed by it; I am much prettier — the skin is taut, fresh, velvety; the eyes alert and bright. In short, it is curious. What must true love do, if such trifles produce this effect?
