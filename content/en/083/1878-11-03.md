%% 2026-10-01T07:32:31 TR: RESOLVED SOURCE CHANGED (2026-09-28 rebuild) — English re-aligned to the rebuilt French. %%
I went to fetch Mother Gavini; there were people at her house, a Mme de Marcilly, to whom I was introduced, as I was yesterday to Mme de Balincourt. Maman, Dina, the lady and I go walking together. They want to marry me off — but so that no one shall use me to enrich some gentleman, I declare plainly that I am quite willing to marry, with pleasure — but on condition that he is rich, has a fine position, and is handsome; or else an intelligent, remarkable man, etc. As for his character, were he the devil himself, I shall see to that.

%% 2026-09-29T01:01:22 RSR: Restored from tome12.docx ¶1681, Mon Journal t.12 p.149 (missing from original extraction, 2026-09-28 rebuild). Printer's typo corrected: «muisque» → «musique» (Mon Journal t.12 p.149). %%
There is a great deal of feeling in the Marquis d'Ivry's music. He has steeped himself in Shakespeare's *Romeo and Juliet*[^083.0098.1], he adores it, and his music is delightful, though simple and almost colourless at first hearing. Moreover, the staging and the costumes are perfect. One is right in the thick of it, and it is *true to life*: the real Middle Ages instead of the one that is usually shown, the conventional Middle Ages, all *chic*, which is tedious.

What a dreadful bourgeoise Mme Gavini is! She speaks of the Arts in such a profane way that I shall not go if she speaks of them in front of me again. She cites those Rothschild ladies who paint at home and have masters, and she says that I shall be able to do as much when I am married. In that detached tone of the woman of the world, of the *bourgeoise*, there is something frightfully brutal that puts every artistic and elevated feeling to shame.
