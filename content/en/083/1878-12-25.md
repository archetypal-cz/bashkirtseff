# Wednesday, 25 December 1878 — Christmas

%% 083.0462 %%
%% [#Family](../../_original/_glossary/culture/themes/FAMILY.md) %%
%% [#Papa](../../_original/_glossary/people/family/PAPA.md) %%
%% Mon père déjeune chez nous. Il m'ennuie tout à fait et j'espère qu'il sent toute ma froideur. %%
My father lunches with us. He bores me completely, and I trust he feels all my coldness.

%% Je suis allée me promener à pied dans les Champs-Elysées avec Paul, nous avons accroché Fillippini en route. Monsieur-père était toujours là lorsqu'arriva Mme Gavini nous remercier de nos jolies bonbonnières. Je suis fâchée d'avoir manqué le spectacle d'hier. Il y avait Canrobert, Fleury, de Bourgoin, Duruy. Et un mardi au Français. Enfin nous faisons des projets d'amusements avec elle et à six heures nous, la famille seulement, sommes menés dîner au Café de Paris. %%
%% 2026-09-29T01:01:22 RSR: Restored from tome12.docx ¶2040, Mon Journal t.12 pp.180–181 (missing from original extraction, 2026-09-28 rebuild). %%
I went walking in the Champs-Élysées with Paul; we picked up Filippini on the way. Monsieur my father was still there when Mme Gavini arrived to thank us for our pretty *bonbonnières*. I am sorry to have missed yesterday's performance. Canrobert, Fleury, de Bourgoin and Duruy were there. And a Tuesday at the Français. In short, we are making plans for amusements with her, and at six o'clock we, the family only, are taken to dine at the Café de Paris.

I put on a mask of nonchalance so as not to take notice of all the unpleasant or hurtful allusions of this personage, whom I endure for Paul's sake. He does not seem to understand it. At eight o'clock I go home with Dina. He bores me, this useless father, and... yes, he is almost as incoherent as my mother. The peacemaking interventions of that wife (all of it for Paul's sake) are not happy ones; I have no need of anyone to explain me or to soften me — I express myself very plainly!

This man fills me with despair at life; his words freeze me and have the effect of a *spell*. I hate him absolutely. I would like to crush him when he puts on his airs of good-natured commiseration. He pities me. The monster, the coward, the hypocrite! Do you know what every gesture, every word, every movement of his says? — Poor blind child, you are walking towards misery and despair; when you get there, I shall hold out my hand to you.

He said it to me word for word at the Grand Hôtel two years ago. He dared to tell everyone, and to say it to my mother and to my very face, that I had never been so happy as at Gavronzi, that I would remember that stay as a paradise. He knows, the liar, the scoundrel, that he is lying, and that I curse him, and my journey, and his house, and him a hundred times over! He wanted to marry me to that fat Gorpintchenko! I could strangle him. He also wanted to marry me to his sister's lover; he told Maman so, adding that Sophie would consent to it on condition that we stayed at her house and that things went on as usual.

I believe my mother threw something at his head, but he was not moved by it; he shrugged his shoulders and looked as if he pitied us. His household and his sisters are a heap of depravity. His sisters are the friends of his mistresses; the little Princess Eristoff was brought up with his bastards and patronised by the French actress. The old princess keeps an Italian coxcomb; Gorpintchenko's mother consorts with hotel waiters... None of this was invented for me here. I am old enough to know what is true. I am afraid you may think my head is being turned against him. Besides, what I am telling is known to everyone. Paul tells it in moments of pique, and then the poor child explains it away and offers excuses so sickening that they need no labels; one knows they come from the Father.
