# Saturday, 15 November 1879

%% 086.0532 %%
%% [#Paris](../../_original/_glossary/places/cities/PARIS.md) [#Tony_Robert_Fleury](../../_original/_glossary/people/mentioned/TONY_ROBERT_FLEURY.md) [#Boulanger](../../_original/_glossary/culture/history/BOULANGER.md) %%
%% Ma peinture va beaucoup mieux. A trois heures je vais chez Tony et tout en cherchant son adresse chez M. Boulanger (le peintre) il m'arrive de passer devant le 10 bis de la rue de Boulogne, Boulanger étant au 6 bis. Tony est vraiment très bon, il ne m'a pas dit grand chose de plus que l'autre jour, mais avec des amplifications et puis il m'a fait les honneurs de son atelier et s'est mis à ma disposition pour tout ce que je voudrais avec une bonne grâce vraiment charmante et approuvant fort la location de l'atelier. Il promet même de venir voir pour les arrangements, l'éclairage etc. Quand il arrive à l'atelier il ne dit ni bonjour ni adieu, aussi ai-je eu envie de rire lorsqu'il m'a descendu les toiles qu'il me donne à copier jusqu'à ma voiture en tirant son chapeau. %%
%% 2026-03-09T15:50:00 TR: "10 bis" — French address suffix meaning 10B; building added between existing numbers %%
%% 2026-03-09T15:50:00 TR: "fait les honneurs de" — showed me around with pride; formal hospitality expression %%
%% 2026-03-09T15:50:00 TR: "tirant son chapeau" — tipping/raising his hat; 19th c. formal male greeting gesture %%
My painting is going much better. At three o'clock I go to Tony's, and while looking for his address at M. Boulanger's (the painter) I happen to pass 10 bis rue de Boulogne, Boulanger being at 6 bis. Tony is really very kind; he did not tell me much more than the other day, but at greater length, and then he showed me round his studio and put himself at my disposal for anything I might want, with a truly charming good grace, strongly approving of my renting the studio. He even promises to come and see about the arrangements, the lighting, etc. When he comes into the atelier he says neither good morning nor goodbye, so I wanted to laugh when he carried the canvases he is giving me to copy down to my carriage and raised his hat.

I am writing with my latest painting before my eyes... it is the first one to stir a certain emotion in me. It is still very weak but there is already something that sings in it...

%% Ce soir dîner. M. et Mme Gavini, M. de Morgan, le comte Chaudordy, M. Géry, la Princesse et ses deux fils. Bojidar vient d'être reçu bachelier. Le dîner est très gai, la soirée aussi pendant laquelle arrivent M. et Mme Berthe. Moi je me fais faire la cour par les vieux, je coquette, je parle d'Emile de Girardin, de politique, je m'insinue, j'intrigue... Gavini et Géry me font comprendre bien des choses d'"Eugène Rougon" de Zola. Une femme qui n'a rien à perdre est une créature puissante et on peut faire bien des choses avec des hommes mûrs... %% 2026-03-09T15:50:00 TR: "recu bachelier" — passed the baccalaureat exam; French secondary education completion %%
%% 2026-03-09T15:50:00 TR: "faire la cour" — to court, to pay romantic attention; Marie flirts strategically %%
%% 2026-03-09T15:50:00 TR: "Eugene Rougon" — Zola's novel "Son Excellence Eugene Rougon" (1876) about political manipulation %%
%% 2026-03-09T15:50:00 TR: "entortiller" — to wrap around one's finger, to manipulate %%
Dinner this evening: M. and Mme Gavini, M. de Morgan, Count Chaudordy, M. Géry, the Princess and her two sons. Bojidar has just passed his *baccalauréat*. The dinner is very gay, and so is the evening, during which M. and Mme Berthe arrive. I get the old men to pay court to me, I flirt, I talk of Émile de Girardin, of politics, I insinuate myself, I intrigue... Gavini and Géry make me understand a good many things in Zola's *Eugène Rougon*. A woman who has nothing to lose is a powerful creature, and one can do a great deal with men of a certain age... Look, if I wished, and if these two old men were more important, I could twist them round my finger very easily... But... there it is, it would be pointless, and besides I should be risking too much.

Young Gabriel did not come, pleading his examination for embassy secretary, but his father and old Mme Gavini bluntly reveal that he has something on his neck which had to be lanced. A silly complaint, in short; I know the name, but I can neither say nor write those common, repulsive things that everyone says everywhere.

Last year Multedo had the same thing, and he came with a little square of court plaster on the nape of his neck to tell Maman that it had been cut that morning and that he had seen his blood flow; and as Maman said nothing, he added fatuously that it was blood "red, brilliant, rich, powerful". No doubt to persuade her to entrust her daughter's happiness to him.
