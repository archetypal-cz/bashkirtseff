# Thursday, 18 December 1879
%% Voici enfin la Fête tant annoncée de la presse Française au profit des inondés de Murcie et des pauvres de Paris. Nous y allons en bande. Mme de Bailleul, la Mouzay et de Daillens, maman, Dina et moi; Paul (arrivé ce matin de Russie), Blanc, Alexis et Daniel neveu de la Mouzay. Dina et moi sommes très chic, j'ai une robe courte de satin noir avec des paniers de dentelle espagnole noire, corsage à pointe en damas noir; perruque châtain foncé, mantille noire avec un soleil piqué de côté. Des diamants aux oreilles et aux bras (achetés dernièrement et que les amis ne connaissent pas), des perles au cou et des gants au-dessus du coude. Bas à jour et souliers noirs. Loup de velours. Nous nous perdons les uns les autres en arrivant. %%
%% 2026-09-29T01:01:34 RSR: Restored from tome13.docx ¶1842, Mon Journal t.13 p.137 (missing from original extraction, 2026-09-28 rebuild). %%
Here at last is the long-heralded Fête of the French press for the benefit of the flood victims of Murcia and the poor of Paris. We go in a band: Mme de Bailleul, la Mouzay and de Daillens, Maman, Dina and I; Paul (arrived from Russia this morning), Blanc, Alexis and Daniel, la Mouzay's nephew. Dina and I are very chic. I have a short dress of black satin with panniers of black Spanish lace, a pointed bodice of black damask; a dark chestnut wig, a black mantilla with a sun pinned at one side. Diamonds in my ears and on my arms (bought lately, and which our friends do not know), pearls at my throat and gloves above the elbow. Openwork stockings and black shoes. A velvet half-mask. We lose one another as soon as we arrive.

This boy is very well brought up, and besides, to him I am the Virgin Mary in spite of the nonsense I talk. That must have made him uneasy, for that very reason, and then the singing, the music, the crowd, the hot air, the half-naked stall-girls and the swaying hips of the Spanish dancers whom we go to watch by way of a rest; it was then that, half from mischief and half from madness, I beg my escort to put his arm behind my waist to protect me from the crowd, which was pushing us against the guitarists sitting in a circle. The respectful child then says to me those two words which we afterwards turn into a catchphrase: *where?* Where, my little one? Why, there — and I place his arm so that the hand must needs rest on my right hip: go on, then, do not be afraid, it is because of all these people, it means nothing to me, go on — and I found myself almost in his arms; had he put in a penny's worth of his own, I would have been there. It is altogether droll when one is sure of one's man.
