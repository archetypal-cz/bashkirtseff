# Sunday, 18 May 1884
[Crossed out: My family will be the death of me]
Mme Gaillard and Bagnitsky are at dinner. And Emile Bastien, deeply moved by my letter. He greeted me quite differently, and several times I could see him looking at me with tenderness — all perfectly proper. I had written so kindly: "say nothing to anyone to avoid gossip — let it remain between us."

And so here we are, friends — by reason of our shared tenderness for the great Jules. You will see what comes of it.

Bagnitsky tells me (I had been coming back from the Bois, where I had gone with the Saritsky girls who are passing through — the father who once wanted to marry Maman has become a grand personage, and now Maman pays court to his daughters) — he tells me that at the painter Bogoluboff's, people were discussing the Salon, and someone — an artist he would of course not name — said: "*There is a very fine Bastien-Lepage, signed Bashkirtseff.*"

And in the anteroom he begins to console me — that it is absurd, that no one will believe it, and that if my painting resembles his brother's it is only because, like him, I seek truth and am sincere before nature, and so on. Meanwhile I, who felt myself like a guilty party and had nearly begun to think he must be furious with me on account of these slanders.

For it is infamous — for Jules Bastien-Lepage as much as for me! Well, this excellent man consoles me as best he can, reassures me; I am almost glad of it, so afraid was I that all this would make me look as though I had imitated Bastien-Lepage, compromised him, and so on... This man ought to be furious on his brother's behalf. And I regard him as the faithful echo of his brother... Well then, I am very saddened and I lament human infamy a little — so as to be reassured, consoled, and coaxed by Mme Gaillard, Bagnitsky, and Emile; the last two speak to me as they would to a child and end by kissing my hands. At bottom I rather enjoyed inspiring compassion — and especially Emile, who shook me by the arm saying: come now, rise above it. You are a force, and so on. You cannot imagine how much importance I attach to what this young man says.

He is sincerity itself. It is even comical. Sometimes I remark laughingly that I am the greatest of artists, and this charming innocent stops me in all seriousness: "Come now, you cannot think that — why speak like this when one has a genuine great talent?"

He is a little naive sometimes, but he is an artist and his judgements are remarkable — I attribute them, what is more, to Jules, which is perfectly natural.

It is deranged, is it not. Monumental. Suppose, I said to her later, that I were truly, deeply upset by this gossip. Do you imagine your idiotic words will soothe me? With this woman there is simply no way.

In front of Mme Gaillard, in front of Bagnitsky whom the architect barely knows, she begins blurting out the whole story of the doctors, the woman at Somny, Jules's illness — insisting on the details of his suffering and the dangers he faces. To understand what an outrage this is, one must know how much this man adores his brother and what a humiliating and horrible grief it is for him to hear his brother's illness picked over by strangers. He keeps to himself so that no one will speak of it. There [Words blacked out: two illegible lines] Is it pleasant to hear... It is four in the morning. Seeing that I was furious, Dina kept Emile and Bagnitsky as long as she could so that it would be too late for family scenes. It changed nothing... I said all this to them between two and three in the morning, in Maman's room. Can one imagine such a thing! Ah! these people will be the death of me. If I were even asking them to assist me — no, I conjure them on my knees to leave me in peace, nothing more! That is all. Peace, peace, peace! Stay neutral. Neutral — that is all.

That I should have the freedom to complain, to speak of human wretchedness, to philosophise with the people who come here — without being subjected to ridiculous scenes.

On the whole I am flattered by the stir my painting is causing. I am envied, I am slandered — I am somebody. So I am perfectly entitled to pose a little if that is my pleasure. But no — I say in a woeful tone:

— Do you not find it horrible — is there not reason enough to be cast down! I spend six years — the six finest years of my life — working like a convict, seeing no one and enjoying nothing of life! After six years I produce something good, and they say it is *not by me*! The reward of so much labour turns into a dreadful calumny!!!

%% Naturellement je bondis: comment voilà la pâture de la majorité, voilà ce qu'on préfère, *o tempra, o mores !* Voulez-vous parier que maman recommence la tirade Bouguereau ou à peu près. Naturally I leap up: so this is the fodder of the majority, this is what is preferred — *o tempora, o mores!*[^tempora] Do you want to wager that Maman launches into the Bouguereau speech, or something like it? It has already happened over several things. She is afraid I shall shatter, that I shall die from the slightest shock, and in her immense naïveté she tries to protect me by means that will end by giving me a raging fever.

[^tempora]: *O tempora, o mores!* — "O the times, O the customs!" A famous exclamation from Cicero (*In Catilinam*, I.1), used here as a lament over the public's literary taste.

X, Y, or Z arrives and says: you know, the La Rochefoucauld ball was superb. I darken. Maman sees it, and five minutes later recounts as if by chance something that is intended to discredit the ball in my mind — unless she undertakes to prove that the ball never took place at all. It has happened. Childish inventions and subterfuges, and I foam with rage to think they believe I can swallow it!
