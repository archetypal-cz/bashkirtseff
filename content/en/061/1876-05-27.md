But this morning I wrote to Potechine, asking him to post four letters in which I arrange to meet Torlonia, Zucchini, Belmonte, and Antonelli at the exhibition of Semiradovski's painting[^1] on Tuesday between two and three o'clock. All purportedly from women they have loved or who love them: Torlonia's letter speaks of a son, Zucchini's of a blue ribbon, Belmonte's of a quarrel that drew blood, and Antonelli's of a love not quite extinguished. They will all meet there. Potechine is charged with reporting the result to me.

I remembered him and pitied him gently. Why does one feel such a singular pleasure in pitying someone one likes? This feeling of pity almost replaces love — it is even sweeter, more Christian. It is not the Duke I should pity. Oh no — he falls upon me like a hurricane, stuns me, troubles me, drives me mad with his air of superb condescension; he leaves me prostrate before his memory.

That would settle everything; I should no longer have to torment myself; I would be content with the name and the position. Yes… but just think: I should be Countess Antonelli, niece of the great Cardinal (I smile with pleasure), but at every moment I should be eclipsed by Italian princesses and by foreign women worth millions, with their fabulous jewels and their magnificent palaces.

Spent well, that goes as far as the three millions a year of people who do not spend them, or spend them badly. Ah! Why can I not come across one of those Englishmen with millions, since they exist! Since I please so many people, why not one of those!!! The first who suits me, I shall take; I should very much like to wait for one exactly as I want him, but that is not certain; it may never come, and then I should die of rage, reproaching myself bitterly for my absurd stupidity. I gave up people who suited me and I have nothing! That is what I should tell myself. The demon urges me to wait and tells me it will not be in vain. The demon tempts me so as to punish and torture me afterwards.
