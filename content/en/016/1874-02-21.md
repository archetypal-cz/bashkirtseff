We drew nearer still to the queen's pavilion. She took flowers from the *young ladies* who were enclosed in a covered pink pavilion, badly made -- and those young ladies! What frights, what ugly manners, atrocious voices, terrible language. It was painful to see. I took Dina's arm to be freer and not be obliged to follow like a dog, for my aunt does not know how to walk together. Suddenly the baron appears and begins to talk nonsense about princes, princesses, duchesses, countesses, etc. etc., complaining how busy he is, how much he has to do with these sales, these concerts for the poor.

I approach alone the young ladies and their flowers -- mouldy and wilted, or else vulgar and coarse. I buy from Denyson and from the Durands; but what do I see? The Comte de Lambertye is behind these flowers -- he has slipped in like a serpent. I was about to say *that animal*, but he does not merit that name, which suits a man who is tall, well built, and elegant, like Hamilton. The little one smiles always. I do not notice him; I am forced to remain a minute or two near these flowers, waiting for Dina, who was hiding. Then I go to fetch her, lend her five francs, and she goes to buy a bouquet from Lucie Durand. The little one is still there.

This evening they are giving *La Jolie Parfumeuse*; I am dying to go see it. We stop at the Theatre Francais to ask for a box. The count again -- and this time he looks at me with such assurance and a little smile, as if he knew me. I believe he is mad. I cannot otherwise explain these manners. For if I interested him (which I do not admit -- I do not think myself enough to interest a dog; I am wretched), he could have been introduced. Truly I think he is mad.
