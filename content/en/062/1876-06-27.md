# Tuesday, 27 June 1876

Sister Thérèse came to see me this morning and brought me the compliments of the Mother Superior. She stood open-mouthed before the portrait of the Pope with the famous *Pax vobis*[^5] that Visconti obtained for us. — Besides, I said, I also have the photograph of Cardinal Antonelli with his signature — here, look. — Oh! the old nun exclaimed — it is Cardinal Antonelli who is the cardinal protector of our convent; we have two houses in Rome. Oh! she said, examining the photograph — what a saintly face — he is a saint, a saint! And she kissed the portrait.

[^5]: *Pax vobis*: Latin, "Peace be with you" — the papal blessing. A photograph or print of Pius IX bearing this inscription.

— Indeed, I said, biting my lips — he is a saintly man. — Oh! Yes! I went to a side table and taking up a photograph of the dissolute cardinal: — Here, Sister — take this one. She nearly collapsed with thanks.

I made her sit down and, as is my habit, reeled off a heap of nonsense mixed with devotion. Antonelli's worshipper was in the middle of teaching me to say the rosary when Cresci was announced and I had to leave her. Well — it turns out that my convent is under the protection of the cardinal-deacon.[^6]

[^6]: Cardinal-deacon: one of the orders of the College of Cardinals. Cardinal Giacomo Antonelli held this rank throughout his career, having never been ordained a priest.

Having decked myself out in an extra-fine woollen dress, entirely floating and draped, like that of the statue of Agrippina[^7] on the Capitoline — only, instead of putting my arms through the sleeves, I passed them through two openings made a little above the elbow on the inside of the arm (I am expressing this badly, no doubt) so that the sleeves remained floating — I also put on a hat and an alms-purse, and went with all my family to see Collignon off, who leaves at two o'clock. From there we went to Bienmüller's, photographer to My Majesty, and I posed four times.

Taking a fold of my drapery I covered my head with it, which — aided by the height of the hat — produced a charming effect, framing my face in wide, diaphanous, graceful folds. I posed seated on a rock, head resting on my elbow, arm bare, neck bathed in the soft shadow of the white wool. An Agrippina gown, a veil in the manner of a... emancipated nun, Louis XV heels — and seated on a rock! Well, I assure you the effect is charming. After stooping to spend an hour on familiar terms with this photographer and his wife, I returned home and was given *Le Figaro*; the headline of the first article made me smile with surprise; I read it and, cutting it out carefully, I attach it below.

> CARDINAL ANTONELLI
> He said to me with a gracious smile, "I shall be with you in a moment!" I sat down on a dark-coloured sofa. He was putting his signature to papers presented to him by a little young man dressed much like those half-abbés of Paris who enter the masses for the dead in the register. Some large red flower of the kind called succulent — a forced winter bloom — stood on his table. I was twenty-four. Much rain has fallen since then! And the Cardinal Pro-Secretary of State, having at last taken my letter, opened it slowly with an enormous ivory knife and read it no faster; and as he did so, he said to me: "How is that excellent Marquis Costa de Beauregard? — M. Menabrea has just made a fine speech — why does M. de Revel not speak more often? There are three men of whom we are very fond!" As none of these questions seemed to await an answer, I gave none. At last he finished reading; and without looking at me, yet watching me sidelong with his superb black eyes, he said: "How is the Count?"

> To understand how dumbfounded I was, one must know two things: the minister was designating Cavour by his Turin appellation, the Count — analogous to the Roman designation of Antonelli: the Cardinal! Now, I had been entrusted by the principal figures of the Piedmontese Right, several of whom are still living, with a small unofficial mission. It was said, above all, to have been undertaken without Cavour's knowledge. The truth was that Cavour knew of it, and that it had been entrusted to me, in agreement with the Prince de La Tour-d'Auvergne, because I was honoured with the goodwill of the two great adversaries, Cavour and Costa. Moreover, the name of my paternal grandmother was well known in Rome. — I played the simpleton and said: "The Count — which Count, Your Eminence?" — "Why, the Count de Cavour! You saw him before you left?" — "No, Eminence." — I lied like an ambassador. But I hope God will forgive me that lie when He is obliged to excuse the Cardinal's diplomatic lies.

> Then the Cardinal enveloped me in that look which is one of his characteristic traits. His pupil widens; it becomes soft, caressing, veiled — or, better said, coaxing; and yet the look remains ironic: the pupil hides a great burst of laughter, as a little schoolgirl sometimes does in church. The Cardinal's lips, however, do no more than smile. His hands sometimes press yours. They rest on your shoulders, as they did on mine. But this laughing doubt envelops you and chills you. Veiled by an excessive Italian courtesy, this irony never owns itself. You have no right to say to it: "I see through you," for the Cardinal would protest, and rightly, and would take you for an ill-bred stickler. Most people do not notice this look, circular in its gentleness but fixed in its irony, and, delighted with the Cardinal, go away saying to themselves: "What a charming man! How he received me, how he understood me!" But I am like dogs, which always look into the eyes of those who speak to them.

> The Cardinal did not even ask me the questions I expected. He contented himself with writing to Turin. That was the last episode in the attempt at a *connubio*[^9] between Cavour and the Right. I recount this not to magnify myself but to give my Italian portraits their stamp of truth. At twenty, to be sure, I had other cares than political ones. Delighted, like a little boy dressed up as a general, to be carrying out a mission of grave appearance, I should have liked to be received by the Pope in private audience and to have a rosary blessed for my mother, saying that it was for her! The Cardinal did not understand that I cared precious little for Cavour's plans once I was out of the orbit in which that astonishing man fascinated men and even events! > Pius IX, whose portrait I shall draw and whom I have seen at close quarters, has had two political manners: the Rossi manner and the Antonelli manner. Rossi was the man of modern passion with ancient Stoicism. Antonelli is the man of Catholic Stoicism with modern passion. The Pius of 1848, to whom the great liberal voice of Balbo had cried, "Courage, Holy Father!", had shown the modern man beneath the medieval robes of the papacy. Rossi had been the minister of that great Catholic adventure, which ended only with his violent death. Cardinal Antonelli emerged from that tomb. He too had had what he called the *mal'aria*[^10] of 1848. As President of the Consulta, he had solemnly addressed to the Holy Father these absolutely authentic words: "Let Your Holiness ==make the civil progress of the century walk in step with the principles of the Catholic religion; a wonderful union==,[^11] etc." We have never been more liberal here! Fortunately for the Cardinal, the condemnations of the Syllabus are not retroactive. When the French Republic had brought Pius IX back from exile, he found the house ransacked. The religious memories, which are as it were its furniture, lay smashed. — Then Antonelli solemnly burned Rossi's system — not in anger but in sorrow, and with full honours. And beside it, as on the funeral pyre of a Scythian king, they laid its great widow — Liberty.

When it became plain that Napoleon III could not or would not save the patrimony of Saint Peter, the French clergy sang the *Domine salvum*,[^12] etc., less loudly. When the Cardinal felt too weak to defend himself, he attacked. One day Cavour died. "Poor Count Camillo!" said the Cardinal. And he believed himself master. But the King was born of Cavour.

Ah! Niece of the Pope! Think of it — to be something... very great, no doubt; oh! yes — to involve myself in politics, intrigue, everything; to be beside a king, a great man — no doubt a great man! Oh, it is the dream of my life — you know that well enough. And not the slightest sacrifice, for after all... Pietro, Pietro is a charming boy. If only he loves me. The letter is dated the 23rd of June; today is the 27th — five days of waiting, of suffering perhaps, of boredom, of misery. What I want will not be ready until Saturday; he shall wait... poor Pietro.*
