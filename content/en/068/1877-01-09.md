# Tuesday, 9 January 1877
I do not know where to begin — I am broken, annihilated, worse than dead. Death would be a deliverance; I would not welcome it with joy — I would weep for myself... but it would be over at once, whereas in living I die every day.

— Madame Bashkirtseff? he asked.
— No, Monsieur, her daughter; but as my mother is indisposed, I am taking her place.
I sat down, motioning him to a chair...

— I am rather embarrassed, Monsieur. Still... I am going to speak frankly, since that is why we are here. First I made inquiries as to who you were. I was told that M. Pierret was a well-bred man and a gentleman. It was then that my mother wrote to you. Does all this explain nothing to you? Wait.
— I am listening, Mademoiselle.
— So, Monsieur, once again you persist in saying that you know us neither by sight nor by name?
— Except for those two times, once at the theatre, the other on the Promenade — yes, Mademoiselle.
— Ah! very well, and that will make our explanation as easy as can be. Since you do not know me, you cannot have said what... you said.

— Ah! So much the better, Monsieur... I shrank from believing such villainy on the part of a gentleman. Sometimes, too, people will hound those they do not even know. How wrong that is — oh, very wrong! Well, Monsieur, since it was not you, it remains for me to ask your pardon for having disturbed you... but you see, I like to bring things to a clear conclusion, and I thought it better, at the cost of being thought extravagant, to ask you to come here and to speak to you openly, rather than to go gathering what is said in corners.
— Quite true, Mademoiselle; I too like to proceed openly.
— So, Monsieur, forgive me for having disturbed you; as for apologising for having suspected you, I shall do nothing of the kind, seeing that I had neither the right to suspect you nor any reason to be sure of you, not knowing you at all.
— Quite so, Mademoiselle; as for the disturbance, on the contrary, I am very happy, since it gives me the pleasure of speaking with you.
I fell silent. He rose.
— Mademoiselle...
— Monsieur...
It lasted a quarter of an hour or twenty minutes in all.
I went to give my family an account of the interview, and at once Walitsky began telling me what I ought to have said to M. Pierret. It is laughable.
My aunt was coming in just as M. Pierret was going out of the door.
— He looked as if he had just stepped out of a hot bath, she said.
We went out, and into the Skating, to prove that I am a subscriber.
In the evening they took me to see *Kosiki*, a new operetta by Lecocq. Sitting in the darkest corner of the box, I saw nothing, heard nothing.
The General came to see us and found me sad.
— People in Nice concern themselves too much with me, I told him; it annoys me — it is infamous!
Instead of reassuring me, he consoled me! Like Princess Souvoroff, he advised us to be seen with Tutcheff: "since all the infamies come out of her house, it is the only way to prove that they are slanders."
In consoling me he brought me to the point where I would have hanged myself there and then, in the box, if I had only had a rope in my hand.
I am so desperate that I no longer know how to complain.
