# Monday, 16 June 1879
%% Je vais donc vous rendre compte de la mémorable et scandaleuse séance d'aujourd'hui qui comme débordement de violences et de stupidité sera certainement historique. %%
%% 2026-09-29T01:01:30 RSR: Restored from tome13.docx ¶511–514, Mon Journal t.13 pp.35–36 (missing from original extraction, 2026-09-28 rebuild). %%
So I shall give you an account of today's memorable and scandalous session, which, as an outpouring of violence and stupidity, will certainly go down in history.

On arriving at Versailles we take the tramway, and I have the pleasure of seeing that my feet are known and appreciated by the honourable Deputies...

Mme Edmond Adam est dans la loge du Président de la République; je cherche vainement Mme Arnaud. Dans une tribune on aperçoit Mme de Boishébert (la prétendue nouvelle maîtresse de Cassagnac) récemment séparée de M. le marquis de Boishébert, son mari et vivant sous la protection d'un vieil oncle fort riche. J'aimerais bien que cette liaison soit vraie... mais la marquise est longue, anguleuse, peu élégante, les épaules un peu de travers et la figure moutonnière. %% 2026-09-29T01:01:30 RSR: Restored from tome13.docx ¶516, Mon Journal t.13 p.36 (missing from original extraction, 2026-09-28 rebuild). %%
Really, you know, I heard some of them whispering it to one another. So these gentlemen lead us to our seats and the session begins. I have on a hat that suits me, which gives me the nerve to look at the whole universe, and at Cassagnac, as though I had just married a Grand Duke. Madame Edmond Adam is in the box of the President of the Republic; I look in vain for Madame Arnaud. In one of the galleries one can see Madame de Boishébert (Cassagnac's supposed new mistress), recently separated from her husband, the Marquis de Boishébert, and living under the protection of a very rich old uncle. We are in Gambetta's box, which is why I did not find the Acard girl, who was in the diplomatic box. I should very much like this liaison to be true... but the marquise is tall, angular, inelegant, her shoulders a little crooked and her face like a sheep's. Painted eyebrows and a complexion quick to redden. On the whole, a certain *je ne sais quoi* that can please. It concerns, as you know, the Jules Ferry bill.

But at the second sentence the speaker gets carried away and an enormous uproar breaks out. Gambetta is very hot-tempered, and from the very start, for a word that in other circumstances would at most have earned a call to order, he inflicts the maximum. There follows such a din that the spectators in the galleries rise to their feet, breathless... the spectacle is unique. The deputies rush into the hemicycle and insults turn to blows; I see with my own eyes elbowing, punches and perhaps slaps exchanged; some of the madmen are carried off, led away; people throw themselves between the combatants. A regular brawl. Meanwhile Cassagnac, pale and motionless, contemplates the battle from the top of the rostrum. Then the President rises, utters a few words that cannot be heard, gesticulates and puts on his hat. No, never have I seen anything so horrible as that man in that hat, which was too big for him. It gave the man an indescribably sinister and frightening air, [words blacked out: this action, so simple in reality] but owing to the meaning attached to it, it is of striking effect. The battle continues and the galleries are cleared.
