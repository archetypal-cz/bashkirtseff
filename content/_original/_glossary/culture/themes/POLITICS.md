## Thematic Tag: Politics

%% GLO_POLITICS.0002 %%
This thematic tag collects diary paragraphs where Marie discusses political events, politicians, elections, political opinions, political journalism, women's rights, and the intersection of politics with her social world. With 688 references across the diary, it is one of the most frequently used tags, reflecting how deeply political life permeated Marie's existence -- from salon conversations and newspaper reading to her personal engagement with feminism and her infatuations with political figures.

%% GLO_POLITICS.0003 %%
## French Politics in the 1870s-80s

%% GLO_POLITICS.0004 %%
Marie's diary spans the most formative years of the [French Third Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Third_Republic), established in September 1870 after the collapse of Napoleon III's Second Empire in the Franco-Prussian War. The Republic was born in military defeat and civil war -- the [Paris Commune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_Commune) of March-May 1871 left between 10,000 and 15,000 dead in the "Semaine sanglante" (Bloody Week) and created political fractures that shaped French politics for decades. When Marie arrived in France in the early 1870s, the Republic was fragile, contested, and far from certain to survive.

%% GLO_POLITICS.0005 %%
French political life during this period was defined by a three-way struggle between [Bonapartists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonapartism) (supporters of Napoleon's dynasty, seeking a plebiscitary empire), [Monarchists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monarchism_in_France) (themselves divided between Legitimists loyal to the Bourbon line and [Orleanists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orl%C3%A9anist) backing the family of Louis-Philippe), and Republicans of various stripes. In 1876, Bonapartists were still the second-largest faction in the Chamber of Deputies, ahead of the Monarchists. The failure of the three anti-republican camps to unite on a single candidate for the throne -- "There is only one throne, and it cannot be occupied by three" -- ultimately secured the Republic's survival.

%% GLO_POLITICS.0006 %%
### The 16 May 1877 Crisis

%% GLO_POLITICS.0007 %%
The pivotal constitutional crisis of Marie's early Paris years was the [16 May 1877 crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/16_May_1877_crisis) ("le Seize Mai"). President Marshal [Patrice de Mac-Mahon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrice_de_Mac-Mahon), a convinced monarchist, dismissed the republican prime minister Jules Simon and dissolved the Chamber of Deputies, hoping that new elections would return a conservative majority. Marie witnessed the tensions firsthand. On 1 July 1877, she attended a military review in Paris during the crisis and recorded the atmosphere:

%% GLO_POLITICS.0008 %%
> *"A cause de tous ces tripotages politiques, on attendait avec émotion la revue d'aujourd'hui. On espérait des acclamations pour le maréchal, une manifestation dans l'armée enfin. Il n'en fut absolument rien."* (1 July 1877)

%% GLO_POLITICS.0009 %%
The October 1877 elections returned 323 Republicans against 209 royalists, decisively rejecting MacMahon's gambit. Marie followed the results with keen interest: *"Pendant que je dis ces choses abstraites 'la France haletante' attend le résultat des élections. Car c'est aujourd'hui. Le maréchal doit avoir mal dîné ce soir."* (14 October 1877). The crisis permanently established that governments were answerable to parliament, not the president -- the practice of presidential dissolution disappeared until the Fifth Republic.

%% GLO_POLITICS.0010 %%
### The Consolidation of the Republic (1879-84)

%% GLO_POLITICS.0011 %%
MacMahon resigned in January 1879, replaced by the moderate republican [Jules Grevy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jules_Gr%C3%A9vy). The early 1880s saw the Republic's consolidation: the amnesty of Communards (1880), the Press Law guaranteeing press freedom (1881), the Ferry laws establishing free, compulsory, secular primary education (1881-82), and the legalization of trade unions (1884). Marie lived through all of this, though her engagement was more with personalities than legislation.

%% GLO_POLITICS.0012 %%
## The Political Press

%% GLO_POLITICS.0013 %%
Political journalism in the 1870s-80s was a blood sport. The daily press exploded from a combined circulation of 1 million in 1870 to 5 million by 1910, and newspapers were the primary arena of political combat. The [Press Law of 29 July 1881](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_the_press_in_France#Press_Law_of_29_July_1881) abolished prior censorship and most press offenses, unleashing a golden age of polemical journalism.

%% GLO_POLITICS.0014 %%
The newspapers Marie read and discussed include:

%% GLO_POLITICS.0015 %%
- **Le Pays** -- The Bonapartist organ edited by [Paul de Cassagnac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Adolphe_Marie_Prosper_Granier_de_Cassagnac). Marie read it avidly, both for political content and because of her fascination with its editor. She quotes its articles directly in her diary and follows its political battles closely (e.g., the Gent affair of April 1878).
- **[Le Figaro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Figaro)** -- The moderate, society-oriented daily. Marie cites its reporting on Bonapartist demonstrations (January 1878) and social events. The columnist "Etincelle" (Baroness de Poilly) was a figure in Marie's social circle.
- **[La Citoyenne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Citoyenne)** -- The feminist newspaper founded by [Hubertine Auclert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hubertine_Auclert) in February 1881. Marie contributed articles and invested 500 francs to become a shareholder (see "Marie and Feminism" below).
- **La France** -- Republican newspaper that Marie also followed, noting its responses to Le Pays.

%% GLO_POLITICS.0016 %%
Political journalism was inseparable from personal combat. Cassagnac fought numerous duels provoked by his articles. The press was how political reputations were made and destroyed, and Marie understood this -- she followed newspaper feuds with the same avidity she brought to salon gossip.

%% GLO_POLITICS.0017 %%
## Marie's Political Education

%% GLO_POLITICS.0018 %%
Marie's political consciousness developed through several channels. She was an omnivorous reader who studied history systematically -- *"J'ai tenu à suivre les différents peuples depuis leur naissance et j'ai commencé l'étude de l'histoire par la Bible, puis Hérodote, Tite-Live, Diodore de Sicile"* (2 July 1876). By 1883 she was reading Michelet's *Histoire de la Revolution francaise* nightly, which gave her ammunition to defend the Revolution in royalist salons.

%% GLO_POLITICS.0019 %%
Her primary political education, however, came through people: Cassagnac's conversation and journalism, the politically charged atmosphere of Parisian salons, the debates at Hubertine Auclert's *Droit des femmes* meetings, and the dinner-table talk of her cosmopolitan circle. She absorbed politics as spectacle, personality, and conversation rather than through systematic ideological commitment.

%% GLO_POLITICS.0020 %%
Marie was also politically formed by her dual identity as a Russian subject living in France. She could view French politics with the detachment of a foreigner while being passionately engaged in Russian affairs (see "International Politics" below). This gave her an unusual vantage point -- she could mock Gambetta at a princess's dinner table, then analyze the French Revolution at a royalist salon, then attend feminist meetings under a pseudonym.

%% GLO_POLITICS.0021 %%
## Marie's Political Opinions

%% GLO_POLITICS.0022 %%
Marie's political views evolved substantially over the diary's twelve years. She defies easy categorization -- her positions were often contradictory, driven as much by personal attraction as by ideology.

%% GLO_POLITICS.0023 %%
### The Bonapartist Phase (1876-79)

%% GLO_POLITICS.0024 %%
Marie's earliest sustained political engagement was through Bonapartism, inseparable from her infatuation with Paul de Cassagnac. She attended the memorial mass for Napoleon III (January 1878) and was moved by the Bonapartist demonstration, even though she did not attend in person: *"'Le Pays' à plusieurs reprises avait prié ses amis d'éviter toute espèce de manifestation. On n'a point crié en effet, mais on a fait une telle manifestation, on a rendu hommage à l'homme et au parti d'une façon si saisissante et si simple que je suis presque contente de n'y avoir pas été, j'en aurais été affolée."* (15 January 1878). She idealized Cassagnac himself: *"Cassagnac est le chef du parti bonapartiste, Cassagnac est un des hommes éminents du jour, par son honnêteté, par son courage, par son éloquence et son immense influence sur les siens. Et si Napoléon IV revient... Bigre !"* (24 July 1876).

%% GLO_POLITICS.0025 %%
### The Republican Awakening (1880-83)

%% GLO_POLITICS.0026 %%
By 1880, Marie's political sympathies were shifting toward republicanism, driven partly by her growing admiration for [Gambetta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%A9on_Gambetta) and partly by her feminist engagement. She records with characteristic honesty how Gambetta's eloquence made Cassagnac seem small: *"Apres un discours de Gambetta je ne pouvais plus retrouver les traces du bonapartiste dans qui s'appelle mon coeur... Il me paraissait petit, mesquin, absent, evapore; je ne comprenais meme pas comment j'avais cru aimer ce monsieur quand il y avait sur la terre des genies comme Gambetta."* (24 January 1883).

%% GLO_POLITICS.0027 %%
At Etincelle's royalist salon in January 1883, Marie defended the French Revolution -- *"timidement mais en termes assez precis une modeste apologie de la Revolution"* -- drawing on her nightly reading of Michelet. When asked if she held republican views (*"Comment, republicaines?"*), she diplomatically praised the Revolution's noble intentions while acknowledging its excesses. By January 1883, reflecting on Prince Jerome's arrest for his manifesto calling for a plebiscite, she blurred the lines entirely: *"Il est d'un imperialisme loyal et liberal qui plait assez, enfin c'est la republique avec un empire hereditaire... Ah ! si Gambetta vivait."* (17 January 1883).

%% GLO_POLITICS.0028 %%
### Politics as Personal Drama

%% GLO_POLITICS.0029 %%
Marie was characteristically self-aware about the role of personality in her politics. In a remarkable passage from July 1883 analyzing "crystallization" (borrowing Stendhal's term for the process of falling in love), she acknowledged that her Bonapartism had been inseparable from her attraction to Cassagnac: *"Il y avait commencement de cristallisation pour Cassagnac, je le raisonne, l'examine, c'est un tribun tapageur, l'Europe en parle, les femmes en sont folles, il est reçu par le Faubourg Saint-Germain. Je m'accorde la permission de l'aimer."* (15 July 1883). Soutzo recalled that his first impression of Marie at a princess's dinner was of a young woman who was *"blaguer Gambetta, parler politique comme un bas-bleu, puis se moquer très sérieusement de tout"* (6 May 1880) -- mocking Gambetta and talking politics like a bluestocking.

%% GLO_POLITICS.0030 %%
## Key Political Figures in the Diary

%% GLO_POLITICS.0031 %%
### Paul de Cassagnac (1843-1904)

%% GLO_POLITICS.0032 %%
[Paul Adolphe Marie Prosper Granier de Cassagnac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Adolphe_Marie_Prosper_Granier_de_Cassagnac) was the dominant political personality in Marie's diary. A Bonapartist journalist, deputy for the Gers (elected 1876), editor of the newspaper *Le Pays*, and famous duellist, Cassagnac was one of the most notorious public figures of the early Third Republic. He took over *Le Pays* in 1866 and used it as a weapon of Bonapartist polemic, fighting both Republicans and Monarchists. He served in the Franco-Prussian War, was captured at Sedan, and returned to political journalism after captivity.

%% GLO_POLITICS.0033 %%
Marie's relationship with Cassagnac was the most sustained political-romantic entanglement of her youth. She first met him socially in Nice in 1876, visited his apartment in Paris in the famous "five women" episode (January 1878), and followed his career obsessively through *Le Pays*. The visit to his apartment -- where she jumped on his bed, washed her face in his basin, and knocked over his silver -- is one of the most vivid episodes in the diary. She described his home as *"l'intérieur d'un Joseph Balsamo quelconque, d'un homme à bonnes fortunes, panaché de politique, de dévouements, de toutes sortes de choses merveilleuses"* (23 January 1878). By 1880, attending the Chamber of Deputies, she noted his physical decline: *"Cassagnac a pris la parole Dieu sait pourquoi. Il a pris du ventre et se fane décidément"* (28 May 1880). By 1883, her disillusionment was complete: *"Ce Cassagnac est vraiment une nature basse"* (5 August 1883).

%% GLO_POLITICS.0035 %%
### Leon Gambetta (1838-1882)

%% GLO_POLITICS.0036 %%
[Leon Gambetta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%A9on_Gambetta) was the great republican leader who proclaimed the Third Republic in 1870, organized resistance during the Franco-Prussian War, and became the most influential politician of the Republic's early years. He served briefly as prime minister (November 1881-January 1882) and died on 31 December 1882, possibly from complications of an accidental gunshot wound, at his house in Ville-d'Avray.

%% GLO_POLITICS.0037 %%
Marie's engagement with Gambetta was audacious. In January 1877, learning that Gambetta was visiting Nice, she sent him a letter: *"Monsieur, Vous ne vous étonnerez sans doute pas d'avoir inspiré à une personne intelligente le plus vif désir de vous connaître et de causer avec vous, aussi vous attendrai-je demain toute la journée chez moi"* (7 January 1877). He never came. Marie's disappointment was crushing: *"Hier j'ai attendu Gambetta et Pierret, ni l'un ni l'autre ne sont venus"* (9 January 1877).

%% GLO_POLITICS.0038 %%
Gambetta's death in January 1883 profoundly affected Marie. She visited his death chamber at Ville-d'Avray with Jules Bastien-Lepage (who was painting the scene), was shocked by the miserable conditions in which he had died -- *"Comment ! C'est dans cette miserable cage dont je touche le plafond avec la main litteralement qu'on a laisse pendant six semaines un malade de la constitution de Gambetta"* (16 January 1883) -- and wept at the bullet hole in the wall. She described him as *"le grand, le noble, le calomnie, le genial Gambetta"* (13 January 1883), and at the Chamber of Deputies mourning session quoted Brisson's eulogy: *"ce chant heroique qui etait la poesie de notre lutteur depuis quinze ans, nous ne l'entendrons plus"* (29 January 1883).

%% GLO_POLITICS.0039 %%
See also: [#Gambetta](../../places/churches/GAMBETTA.md), [#Leon_Gambetta](../../people/mentioned/LEON_GAMBETTA.md)

%% GLO_POLITICS.0040 %%
### Other Political Figures

%% GLO_POLITICS.0041 %%
- **Marshal [MacMahon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrice_de_Mac-Mahon)** (1808-1893) -- President of the Republic 1873-79. Marie saw his wife "faisant l'impératrice" at the 1877 military review. His constitutional crisis shaped the political landscape of her early years in Paris.
- **[Georges Clemenceau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_Clemenceau)** (1841-1929) -- Rising republican leader. Marie attended his interpellation in the Chamber (May 1880) and assessed him as a "petit monsieur mathematique" compared to Gambetta's genius.
- **[Clovis Hugues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clovis_Hugues)** (1851-1907) -- Republican poet and deputy, a former Communard. Marie dined with him and was charmed by his political imitations of Gambetta, Madier, and Clemenceau (May 1883): *"Ces communards sont charmants."*
- **Emile de Girardin** (1802-1881) -- Veteran journalist and political figure. Marie met him at Mme de Brimont's salon (December 1880) and was thrilled: *"l'émotion de voir enfin de près un de ces hommes qui tient le haut du pavé de la politique ou du journalisme!"*
- **Prince Jerome Napoleon** -- Bonapartist pretender. Marie followed his arrest in January 1883 for a manifesto calling for a plebiscite.

%% GLO_POLITICS.0042 %%
## Marie and Feminism

%% GLO_POLITICS.0043 %%
Marie's feminist engagement represents her most concrete political activism. Beginning in late 1880, she became involved with [Hubertine Auclert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hubertine_Auclert) (1848-1914), the pioneering suffragist who founded the organization *le Droit des femmes* (Women's Rights) in 1876 and the newspaper [La Citoyenne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/La_Citoyenne) in February 1881. Auclert is credited with coining the word "feminist" in 1882.

%% GLO_POLITICS.0044 %%
### The Visit to Auclert (December 1880)

%% GLO_POLITICS.0045 %%
On 1 December 1880, Marie visited Auclert at 12 rue du Cail with her friend de Daillens. The visit was preceded by reading Emile de Girardin's pamphlet *"L'égale de l'homme"* and a resolution to prepare herself for a new world: *"Il faut que je sois au moins, au moins bachelière ès lettres quand la femme sortira de son humiliation"* (21 November 1880). At Auclert's modest headquarters -- *"Droit des femmes siège social. Les mots écrits sur la porte m'avaient déjà donné... un accès d'enthousiasme"* -- Marie adopted the pseudonym "Pauline Daria Orell" and joined the organization. She noted Auclert's appearance with a painter's eye: *"Brune, le teint un peu couperosé peut-être... De petites mains un peu rouges, de petits pieds. Une mise et un langage très convenables"* (1 December 1880).

%% GLO_POLITICS.0046 %%
### The Feminist Meetings

%% GLO_POLITICS.0047 %%
Marie attended weekly meetings at Auclert's headquarters wearing a disguise (black wig, darkened eyebrows, pince-nez) to protect her identity. Her description of the 8 December 1880 meeting is both vivid and revealing of her class tensions: *"Une vingtaine de vieilles typesses, des espèces de concierges en rupture de loges et quelques hommes, le rebut de ce que l'on s'imagine"* -- yet she recognized that *"Hubertine est très sage et comprend qu'il ne s'agit ni de prolétaires ni de millionnaires, mais de la femme en général qui revendique ses droits."*

%% GLO_POLITICS.0048 %%
Her most impassioned feminist passage is from 2 December 1880, where she rages against the women of her own atelier who reject emancipation: *"Pensez-donc sur les quinze femmes de chez Julian il n'y en a pas une qui ne rirait, ou ne se signerait, à l'idée de l'émancipation de la femme... On ne force pas la femme à aller pérorer dans les cafés mais nous voulons qu'elle soit libre, de s'adonner à la carrière qui lui convient le mieux."*

%% GLO_POLITICS.0049 %%
### La Citoyenne and Marie's Articles

%% GLO_POLITICS.0050 %%
Marie invested 500 francs to become a shareholder in *La Citoyenne* (6 March 1881), and published articles in the newspaper under the pseudonym "Pauline Orell." Her article about women artists appeared on 20 February 1881, arguing: "What we need is the possibility to work like men." She also wrote a Salon review for the newspaper (May 1881). She recorded seeing her first article in print with satisfaction: *"Ça m'empêche d'être contente de voir mon article imprimé dans La Citoyenne. Lisez aussi celui de Hubertine qui est très remarquable"* (7 March 1881) -- the "empêche" referring to other troubles, not to displeasure with the article itself. She assessed Auclert as *"extrêmement modérée"* and lamented that conservative newspapers always linked her with [Louise Michel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louise_Michel), the radical Communard: *"Ce que c'est que les clichés, la routine"* (16 February 1881).

%% GLO_POLITICS.0051 %%
### Marie's Feminism and Class

%% GLO_POLITICS.0052 %%
Marie's feminism was sincere but shot through with class contradictions. She advocated for women's rights at dinner parties -- *"La politique a eu sa part aussi et j'ai revendiqué les droits de la femme. C'est la plus grande des injustices à l'heure qu'il est"* (March 1883) -- and had articulated a sophisticated gender philosophy as early as 1877: *"L'homme c'est la force, la femme c'est l'intelligence"* (9 January 1877). Yet she attended feminist meetings in disguise, feared her family's reaction, and was often repelled by the social class of her fellow activists. Her feminism was ultimately grounded in personal experience -- the barriers she faced as a woman painter -- more than in abstract ideology.

## International Politics

%% GLO_POLITICS.0055 %%
### The Russo-Turkish War (1877-78)

%% GLO_POLITICS.0056 %%
As a Russian subject, Marie was passionately engaged with the [Russo-Turkish War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russo-Turkish_War_(1877%E2%80%931878)). Even before Russia officially declared war in April 1877, she witnessed the volunteer movement during her Russian travels in summer 1876. In Moscow, she saw officers departing for Serbia and recorded a visceral pro-Slavic, anti-Turkish outburst: *"On ne peut que se sentir émue de pitié pour nos frères qu'on laisse tranquillement égorger et couper par morceaux par ces affreux sauvages de Turquie, par cette nation sans génie, sans civilisation, sans morale, sans gloire"* (11-12 August 1876). She kept a proclamation from the Moscow Slavic Committee calling for aid to the Serbs.

%% GLO_POLITICS.0057 %%
During the war itself, Marie followed the news anxiously: *"Vous croyez que je ne suis pas inquiète de la Russie ?! Quel est l'être assez malheureux, assez méprisable... pour oublier sa patrie en danger ?!"* (7 August 1877). Her household domestics turned war bulletins into comic dispatches, with Walitsky dubbing the footman Fortuné "Stoïko Pacha" and composing mock military telegrams. Marie's Russian patriotism coexisted with her anti-revolutionary conservatism: she feared that war might give nihilists the opportunity to create *"une petite Commune pour commencer"* (August 1876).

%% GLO_POLITICS.0058 %%
### Russian Internal Politics

%% GLO_POLITICS.0059 %%
Marie held conservative views on Russian politics, fearing the nihilist movement and supporting the autocracy. In a remarkable passage from August 1876, she imagined herself as Tsar, addressing the Russian people from a tribune to warn them against revolutionary agitators -- *"des Gambettas insensés"* -- using the French republican leader as a synonym for dangerous radicalism, even as she was becoming politically attracted to republicanism in France. This double political consciousness -- conservative Russian imperialist and increasingly liberal Francophone intellectual -- is one of the diary's most fascinating tensions.

%% GLO_POLITICS.0060 %%
## Political Salons

%% GLO_POLITICS.0061 %%
Parisian political life in the 1870s-80s was inseparable from the [salon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salon_(gathering)) culture. Marie encountered politics primarily through these social gatherings:

%% GLO_POLITICS.0062 %%
- **Mme de Brimont's Thursdays** -- Regular receptions that attracted *"beaucoup d'hommes politiques et d'autres très distingués"* (16 December 1880). It was here that Marie met Emile de Girardin, the veteran political journalist.
- **Etincelle's salon** -- The Baroness de Poilly ("Etincelle," society columnist for Le Figaro) hosted in a pure Louis XVI salon where Marie defended the French Revolution against Orleanist invective (January 1883).
- **[Mme Adam's salon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juliette_Adam)** -- Juliette Adam (1836-1936), republican salon hostess, editor of *La Nouvelle Revue*, and political figure in her own right. Marie knew of her and her circle.
- **Artistic-Republican dinners** -- Marie attended dinners where politics and art mingled, such as the evening with Clovis Hugues and Anatole de la Forge where political imitations and feminist declarations were mixed with poetry and music (May 1883).
- **The Chamber of Deputies** -- Marie attended sessions of the Chamber as a spectator, notably Clemenceau's interpellation (May 1880) and the Gambetta mourning session (January 1883).

## Usage Notes

The `#Politics` tag should be applied to paragraphs containing:
- Discussion of political events, elections, legislation, or government actions
- Mentions of or commentary on political figures (Cassagnac, Gambetta, MacMahon, Clemenceau, etc.)
- Reading or quoting of political newspapers (Le Pays, La Citoyenne, Le Figaro on political topics)
- Attendance at the Chamber of Deputies or other political institutions
- Political discussions at salons or dinner parties
- Women's rights activism and feminist engagement
- International political events (wars, diplomacy, colonial matters)
- Commentary on political ideologies (Bonapartism, republicanism, socialism, feminism)
- Marie's own political opinions and self-analysis of her political positions
