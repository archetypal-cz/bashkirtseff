## Thematic Tag: Health & Illness

%% GLO_HEALTH.0002 %%
This thematic tag collects diary paragraphs where Marie discusses health, illness, medical treatment, doctors, and bodily symptoms. Health is the diary's shadow narrative: at first barely audible beneath Marie's accounts of love, ambition, and social life, it grows steadily louder until, in the final years, it becomes the dominant voice -- the ticking clock against which everything else is measured. With 1,069 references across the diary, it is one of the most frequently tagged themes, reflecting how completely illness came to permeate Marie's existence.

%% GLO_HEALTH.0003 %%
## Medicine in the 1870s-1880s

%% GLO_HEALTH.0004 %%
Marie lived through one of the most transformative periods in the history of medicine -- yet she died of a disease for which no effective treatment would exist for another sixty years. Understanding the medical world she inhabited is essential to understanding her diary.

%% GLO_HEALTH.0005 %%
### The State of Medical Knowledge

Before the 1880s, the dominant theory of disease was still shifting from [miasma theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miasma_theory) (disease caused by "bad air") to [germ theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germ_theory_of_disease). Louis Pasteur's work on fermentation and vaccination in the 1860s-70s had begun to establish that microorganisms caused disease, but it was [Robert Koch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Koch)'s identification of *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* on 24 March 1882 that proved tuberculosis was caused by a specific bacterium, not by heredity, climate, or temperament as was widely believed. Koch's discovery -- announced to the Berlin Physiological Society -- was a landmark that earned him the 1905 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

%% GLO_HEALTH.0006 %%
Yet Koch's discovery, which occurred during Marie's lifetime, changed almost nothing for patients like her. Understanding the cause of tuberculosis did not produce a cure. Effective antibiotic treatment ([streptomycin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streptomycin)) would not arrive until 1944, and the BCG vaccine was not widely used until the 1920s. In Marie's world, diagnosis was essentially a death sentence -- slow, perhaps, but certain.

%% GLO_HEALTH.0007 %%
### The Medical Profession

Medicine in 1880s Paris was practiced through house calls and office consultations. Doctors examined patients primarily through [auscultation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auscultation) (listening to the lungs with a stethoscope), percussion, and observation. There were no X-rays (Rontgen's discovery came in 1895), no blood tests, no bacteriological confirmation available to clinicians. Diagnosis depended on the physician's ear, experience, and clinical intuition. Marie describes being "auscultee" repeatedly, with doctors listening for the telltale sounds -- crackling, wheezing, diminished breath sounds -- that indicated lung disease.

%% GLO_HEALTH.0008 %%
The doctor-patient relationship was hierarchical and paternalistic, particularly with women patients. Diagnoses were routinely softened or concealed. Marie's Dr. Potain "n'a jamais voulu dire que les poumons etaient atteints, il employait les formules ordinaires en pareil cas, les bronches, la bronchite etc." (28 December 1882). The euphemistic diagnosis -- "peri-bronchite chronique" rather than "phtisie" -- was standard practice, meant to spare the patient (and her family) the full horror of what was then an incurable disease.

%% GLO_HEALTH.0009 %%
### Women's Health in the 1880s

Women patients faced additional obstacles. The medical profession was overwhelmingly male, and women's complaints were frequently attributed to nervous temperament, [hysteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hysteria), or emotional excess. Marie's family treated her symptoms as products of her difficult personality -- "un bebe malade" who needed to be sheltered from the truth. Her aunt attributed her coughing to imprudence; her family's idea of care was to wrap her in flannel and restrict her movements, while ignoring her desperate desire for honest information about her condition.

%% GLO_HEALTH.0010 %%
## Tuberculosis: The White Plague

%% GLO_HEALTH.0011 %%
[Tuberculosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuberculosis) was the leading cause of death in 19th-century Europe, responsible for roughly one in four deaths. Known as "the white plague" (for the extreme pallor of its victims) or simply "consumption" (the body appeared to be consumed from within), it was an omnipresent reality. See [History of tuberculosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_tuberculosis) for the full historical picture.

%% GLO_HEALTH.0012 %%
### The Romantic Mythology

TB held a peculiar cultural status in the 19th century. It was the "[Romantic disease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuberculosis_in_human_culture)" -- associated with artistic genius, heightened sensitivity, and ethereal beauty. The slow wasting was seen as a spiritual refinement, the pallor as otherworldly grace. John Keats, Frederic Chopin, all three Bronte sisters, and countless other artists died of it, reinforcing the mythology that TB and creative genius were linked. As Susan Sontag would later argue in *Illness as Metaphor* (1978), TB became "a way of affirming the value of being more conscious, more complex psychologically."

%% GLO_HEALTH.0013 %%
Marie herself fit this archetype with devastating precision: a young woman of extraordinary talent and ambition, consumed by the very disease that Romantic culture had glamorized. The irony was not lost on her. She oscillated between horror at her condition and a kind of dark romanticism about it -- bargaining with God for "encore dix ans et pendant ces dix annees de la gloire et de l'amour et je mourrai contente a trente ans. Non a trente-cinq" (28 December 1882).

%% GLO_HEALTH.0014 %%
### The Grim Reality

The reality of tuberculosis was anything but romantic. The disease attacked the lungs (and often the larynx, bones, and other organs), producing chronic coughing, bloody sputum, fever, night sweats, weight loss, and eventually death by suffocation or hemorrhage. It was contagious, though this was not understood until Koch's discovery, and even then the mechanisms of transmission were debated. Marie's family history included tuberculosis: "le pere de grand-papa et ses deux soeurs la comtesse de Toulouse-Lautrec et Mme Stralbo. Un arriere grand-pere et deux grandes tantes" (28 December 1882) -- a hereditary pattern that predisposed her, though the hereditary theory was wrong about the mechanism.

## Marie's Medical Timeline

%% GLO_HEALTH.0016 %%
### Early Symptoms (1873-1876)

Health is a background concern in the early diary. Marie mentions occasional colds, minor fevers, and passing indispositions, but these do not yet form a pattern. In Nice as a young teenager, she sometimes feels "une douleur" under the right shoulder blade -- a detail she recalls years later with bitter hindsight: "il y [a] cinq, six ans je sentais parfois comme une douleur a cet endroit, seulement j'etais convaincue qu'il allait me pousser une bosse, ayant deux tantes bossues" (19 October 1880).

%% GLO_HEALTH.0017 %%
### The Voice and the Throat (1876-1879)

Marie's first sustained medical concern is her voice. She aspired to become a professional singer before turning to painting, and persistent laryngitis threatened that dream. By mid-1877 she is seeing Dr. Fauvel in Paris and taking sulfur inhalation treatments at Enghien-les-Bains: "Nous etions chez le Dr Fauvel, il ordonne une cure de quinze jours a Enghien. J'obeis mais... je regardais ma maladie avec de si grands yeux que le traitement qu'il prescrit me parait si peu de chose que je ne crois pas a une guerison prochaine..." (22 June 1877). In October 1877, Fauvel declares her throat cured: "ma gorge est guerie, les cordes vocales sont blanches!" But the relief is temporary. The recurring "crapaud dans la gorge" (toad in the throat) returns, signaling what is actually laryngeal tuberculosis. By October 1880, Marie writes bitterly: "Voila quatre ans que je soigne chez les plus celebres docteurs une laryngite, et cela va de mal en pis" (5 October 1880).

%% GLO_HEALTH.0018 %%
### Hearing Loss and the First Alarm (1880)

In April 1880, the diary records a devastating new symptom. Marie receives a letter from her friend Mouzay warning that her chronic laryngitis could lead to deafness: "gare la surdite" (24 April 1880). Marie is already experiencing tinnitus -- "bourdonnements de mes oreilles" -- that prevents her from hearing her painting teacher Tony Robert-Fleury's critiques. She cannot hear what he says about her painting and must fake comprehension: "il parlait bas et grace aux bourdonnements de mes oreilles je n'ai rien entendu mais j'ai fait semblant" (24 April 1880).

%% GLO_HEALTH.0019 %%
By summer 1880, Marie begins thermal treatments at Mont-Dore for her hearing and throat: "j'ai commence mon traitement... bain, douche, boisson, aspiration... c'est la derniere fois que je me soigne et je ne me soignerais pas si je ne craignais de devenir sourde" (21 July 1880). In September, a doctor confirms the worst: "il y a perforation du tympan dans les deux oreilles et que je n'entendrais plus jamais comme avant. Il y aura toujours comme un voile" (17 September 1880). Marie's response reveals the depths of her despair: "Une fois en Russie j'ai voulu me tuer mais une horrible crainte de l'enfer m'a retenue; hier la peur etait moindre."

%% GLO_HEALTH.0020 %%
### The Bronchial Diagnosis (September 1880)

On 10 September 1880, Dr. Fauvel delivers the news that marks the turning point. After auscultation he finds "les bronches attaquees" and prescribes the standard regimen for a consumptive patient: "huile de foie de morue, badigeonnage a l'iode, lait chaud, flanelle." Marie's reaction is one of the diary's most chilling passages: "Moi, cela m'amuse... je serais contente d'avoir quelque chose de serieux et d'en finir... La mort ne m'effraie pas, je n'oserais pas me tuer, mais je voudrais en finir... je ne mettrai pas de flanelle et ne me salirai pas avec de l'iode. Je ne tiens pas a guerir."

%% GLO_HEALTH.0021 %%
### Euphemisms and Denial (1881-1882)

Throughout 1881, Marie's doctors maintain the fiction of "peri-bronchite chronique" -- chronic inflammation around the bronchial tubes -- rather than naming the disease directly. Marie plays along, vacillating between terror and sardonic humor: "mes assassins de docteurs disent que cela va beaucoup mieux et que je ne merite pas la veine que j'ai" (19 January 1882). In March 1881, after catching cold at a ball, she writes with grim self-knowledge: "Je suis tres malade, je tousse tres fort, respire avec peine et il se fait dans mon gosier un clapotement sinistre... Je crois que cela s'appelle une phtisie laryngee" (3 March 1881).

%% GLO_HEALTH.0022 %%
### Confirmed Tuberculosis (December 1882)

The defining medical moment comes on 28 December 1882. A young doctor -- the one treating her ears -- examines her lungs and tells her the truth: "Je suis poitrinaire. Il me l'a dit aujourd'hui... les deux poumons sont atteints, le gauche beaucoup moins pourtant." Marie notes the family history of consumption, the doctor's astonishment at her healthy appearance ("a mon aspect on ne me donnerait jamais, jamais ma maladie"), and her own eerie calm: "On peut enrayer si je fais ce qu'il faut. C'est-a-dire: des vesicatoires et le midi. Me defigurer les epaules pour un an et m'exiler." She bargains: "qu'on me laisse encore dix ans... de la gloire et de l'amour et je mourrai contente."

%% GLO_HEALTH.0023 %%
### Accelerating Decline (1883-1884)

After the confirmation, health becomes the diary's constant counterpoint. In July 1883, Marie cauterizes her own chest: "je me suis brulee la poitrine, au-dessus du sein droit, a l'endroit ou le poumon est malade... au moins je ne mourrai pas poitrinaire" (22 July 1883). By early 1884, she is losing weight rapidly: "je maigris, je suis deja maigre" (23 January 1884); "J'ai maigri de la moitie. Depuis deux mois on peut suivre jour par jour les progres de cet amincissement" (22 June 1884). The fever is constant: "ce qui est agacant c'est que j'ai la fievre tous les jours" (18 June 1884).

%% GLO_HEALTH.0024 %%
### The Final Consultations (July 1884)

In July 1884, Marie sees multiple doctors in rapid succession. On 3 July she visits Potain at seven in the morning: "Il m'a examinee assez legerement et m'envoie aux Eaux-Bonnes." She intercepts the letter Potain writes to the spa doctor and reads the clinical truth he would not tell her directly: "il y est dit qu'il y a une excavation au sommet droit... que je suis la malade la plus indisciplinee et la plus imprudente du monde." The "petit Beclere" (the young Antoine Beclere, later the father of French radiology) disagrees with Potain's optimism and insists she see Grancher. On 7 July, Grancher "me promet de me guerir en deux annees de traitement vigoureux et rigoureux" -- a promise that could not be kept.

%% GLO_HEALTH.0025 %%
### The Last Weeks (October 1884)

The diary's final medical entry, on 9 October 1884, finds Marie barely able to dress herself: "Vous voyez. Je ne fais rien. J'ai la fievre tout le temps. Mes medecins sont deux jolis imbeciles." She has called Potain back and "me suis remise entre ses pattes." She and the dying Jules Bastien-Lepage sit together under the same blanket, feet on the same hot water bottle -- two brilliant artists consumed by disease. Marie died on 31 October 1884, twenty-two days after her last diary entry, at age twenty-five.

%% GLO_HEALTH.0026 %%
## The Doctors

%% GLO_HEALTH.0027 %%
Marie's medical care involved some of the most eminent physicians in Paris:

%% GLO_HEALTH.0028 %%
- **[Pierre-Carl Potain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Potain)** (1825-1901): France's leading specialist in cardiology and pulmonary disease, professor at the Paris medical faculty, member of the Academie de Medecine. Potain was Marie's primary physician from 1880 onward. He examined her repeatedly, used the euphemistic language standard for the era, and prescribed thermal cures and rest. Marie's attitude toward him swings between gratitude ("il m'a guerie une fois. Il est bon, attentionne, honnete") and fury at his evasions. In December 1882 she notes bitterly that "Potain n'a jamais voulu dire que les poumons etaient atteints."

%% GLO_HEALTH.0029 %%
- **Pierre-Charles Fauvel** (1830-1895): Paris's most famous [laryngologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laryngology), who consulted 19,000 patients over his career, including the Emperor of Brazil and Queen Isabella of Spain. Fauvel treated Marie's throat condition from 1877, prescribing sulfur inhalation cures at Enghien-les-Bains and later iodine treatments. He was the one who, on 10 September 1880, first diagnosed the bronchial involvement that signaled tuberculosis.

%% GLO_HEALTH.0030 %%
- **Maurice Krishaber** (1836-1883): Hungarian-born Paris ENT specialist and professor of laryngeal pathology, known for perfecting the tracheotomy cannula. He treated Marie's throat and advised her on thermal cures (the choice between Mont-Dore and the sea). Marie blamed him for worsening her condition: "Krishaber me soignait et ayant senti je ne sais quoi d'etrange dans les oreilles j'allais le consulter" but was prevented by social complications from returning (11 December 1880). Her reference to "les pharmaciens Krishaber" (20 April 1881) suggests disillusion.

%% GLO_HEALTH.0031 %%
- **Antoine Beclere** (1856-1939): In 1884 still a young doctor ("le petit Beclere"), he would later become the father of [French radiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoine_B%C3%A9cl%C3%A8re), performing the first fluoroscopic screening for pulmonary tuberculosis in 1896. In Marie's diary, he is the earnest young physician who disagrees with Potain's optimism, correctly distinguishing between an earlier acute crisis and the current "aggravation de maladie chronique," and who insists on bringing Marie to Grancher.

%% GLO_HEALTH.0032 %%
- **[Jacques-Joseph Grancher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques-Joseph_Grancher)** (1843-1907): Pediatrician and tuberculosis researcher at the Hopital des Enfants Malades, later a key Pasteurian who participated in the first rabies vaccination (1885). In July 1884 he promised Marie "de me guerir en deux annees de traitement vigoureux et rigoureux" -- an impossible promise given the state of medical science.

%% GLO_HEALTH.0033 %%
- **Unnamed doctors**: Marie refers collectively to her physicians as "mes assassins de docteurs" -- a phrase that captures both her black humor and her genuine rage at their impotence. The unnamed young doctor of the "rue de l'Echiquier" who confirmed her tuberculosis in December 1882 was the one who finally told her the truth she both demanded and dreaded.

%% GLO_HEALTH.0034 %%
## Hearing Loss

%% GLO_HEALTH.0035 %%
Marie's progressive deafness is the diary's most socially devastating medical thread. While tuberculosis would ultimately kill her, it was the hearing loss that most immediately threatened her daily life, her social standing, and her sense of self.

%% GLO_HEALTH.0036 %%
### Progression

The hearing loss appears to have resulted from tuberculosis affecting the Eustachian tubes and middle ear, leading to perforation of both tympanic membranes. The timeline:

- **Early 1880**: Tinnitus ("bourdonnements") begins; Marie cannot hear her painting teacher (carnet 088)
- **April 1880**: Mouzay's nephew warns that untreated laryngitis could cause deafness; Marie admits "voila plus de deux mois que cela a commence" (24 April 1880)
- **May 1880**: Marie struggles at dinner parties, making "les plus grands efforts pour qu'on ne s'apercoive de rien" (13 May 1880)
- **July-August 1880**: Thermal treatments at Mont-Dore; "ma surdite va beaucoup mieux, presque bien" -- temporary improvement
- **September 1880**: Doctor confirms bilateral tympanic perforation and permanent hearing loss (17 September 1880)
- **October 1880**: "Je ne vois personne et le peu de gens que je pourrai voir je les fuis de peur de ne pas entendre ce qu'ils disent" (22 October 1880)
- **November 1882**: Marie finally speaks the word she has been avoiding: "Monsieur je deviens sourde" (16 November 1882)
- **December 1882**: "Devenir sourde?... n'est-ce pas?... il s'agit d'une autre ou bien je vais me reveiller" (1 December 1882)

%% GLO_HEALTH.0037 %%
### Social Impact

The deafness was devastating precisely because it was invisible. Marie could function in noisy environments (salons, theaters) where the general hubbub masked her deficit, but intimate conversation -- the currency of 19th-century social life -- became agonizing: "Au salon, au theatre ca ne se sent pas, mais a deux ou a trois, dans les conversations calmes je suis sourde" (8 August 1882). She lived in constant terror that others would discover her secret: "tout le monde le sait ou va le savoir, tous ceux qui etaient deja si heureux de me decrier... Elle est sourde..." (17 November 1882). The word "sourde" itself horrified her -- she avoided it for years, using euphemisms: "j'ai les oreilles bouchees," "je n'entends pas bien." When she finally pronounced it to a doctor, she marked the moment as a milestone of courage (16 November 1882).

%% GLO_HEALTH.0038 %%
## Thermal Cures and Treatments

%% GLO_HEALTH.0039 %%
In the absence of antibiotics, the primary medical response to tuberculosis was environmental: change the patient's climate, improve nutrition, and hope. Marie underwent the full repertoire of 19th-century treatments. See [Thermal bath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_bath) and [Spa town](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spa_town) for historical context.

%% GLO_HEALTH.0040 %%
### Spa Treatments (Cures Thermales)

- **Enghien-les-Bains** (1877): Sulfur inhalation cure prescribed by Fauvel for laryngitis
- **Schwalbach** (1875) and **Soden** (1877-78): German spa towns; a doctor at the waters found "je ne sais quoi au poumon droit, sous l'omoplate" -- Marie laughed it off
- **Ems** (1878): Consultation with German doctors about her throat
- **Mont-Dore** (1880): Full thermal cure for hearing loss and respiratory problems -- "bain, douche, boisson, aspiration"
- **Eaux-Bonnes** (1884): Pyrenean spa town prescribed by Potain in Marie's final summer, known since the 16th century for treating respiratory conditions

%% GLO_HEALTH.0041 %%
### Medical Treatments

The treatments prescribed for Marie represent the standard (and largely futile) pharmacopoeia of the era:

- **Iodine painting** (badigeonnage a l'iode): Topical application of iodine solution to the chest wall over the affected lung
- **Vesicatories** (vesicatoires): Blistering agents applied to the skin to "draw out" disease -- Marie resisted these fiercely because they left visible marks
- **Cauterization** (pointes de feu): Actual burning of the chest skin; Marie eventually submitted: "je me suis brulee les deux cotes de la poitrine, je ne pourrai plus me decolleter pendant quatre mois" (5 May 1884)
- **Cod liver oil** (huile de foie de morue): Standard nutritional supplement for consumptives
- **Warm milk** (lait chaud) and **goat's milk** (lait de chevre): Believed therapeutic for invalids
- **Flannel**: Worn against the skin to maintain warmth -- Marie's family was obsessed with making her wear it
- **Arsenic**: Mentioned among her medications in the final months
- **Homeopathy**: Marie tried it briefly in 1880 after losing faith in conventional medicine: "J'ai essaye pendant quatre ans de l'allopathie, je vais essayer de l'autre" (14 September 1880)

%% GLO_HEALTH.0042 %%
### The Sanatorium Movement

During Marie's lifetime, the [sanatorium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanatorium) movement was just beginning. Hermann Brehmer had opened the first purpose-built tuberculosis sanatorium in Silesia in 1854, and by the 1880s the concept was spreading across Europe. The idea was that rest, fresh mountain air, good nutrition, and medical supervision could arrest or even cure the disease. Marie was never sent to a sanatorium -- her doctors prescribed the Riviera climate and spa towns instead -- but the principle was the same: remove the patient from urban life and hope that the body's own defenses could prevail.

%% GLO_HEALTH.0043 %%
## Marie's Medical Language

%% GLO_HEALTH.0044 %%
Marie's diary is rich in medical vocabulary, both technical terms learned from her doctors and her own vivid coinages:

### Period Medical Terms
- **poitrinaire** -- consumptive, one suffering from pulmonary disease (from "poitrine," chest)
- **phtisie** / **phtisie laryngee** -- phthisis / laryngeal tuberculosis; the standard medical term, from Greek *phthisis* (wasting)
- **peri-bronchite chronique** -- chronic peri-bronchitis; the euphemistic diagnosis doctors used to avoid saying "tuberculosis"
- **laryngite** -- laryngitis; the early diagnosis that masked the underlying TB
- **bronches attaquees** -- bronchial tubes affected; the language of Fauvel's September 1880 diagnosis
- **excavation au sommet droit** -- cavity at the top of the right lung; the clinical finding Potain reported in his letter (July 1884)
- **auscultee** -- examined by auscultation (stethoscope listening)
- **vesicatoire** -- blistering agent applied to the skin
- **pointes de feu** -- cauterization points
- **badigeonnage a l'iode** -- iodine painting/swabbing
- **sinapisme** -- mustard plaster

### Marie's Own Expressions
- **"mes assassins de docteurs"** -- my murderous doctors (19 January 1882)
- **"je tousse et etouffe"** -- I cough and choke (10 September 1880)
- **"la temperature est toujours a 38,3"** -- precise fever readings, a modern-sounding clinical notation
- **"un crapaud dans la gorge"** -- a toad in the throat (January 1877)
- **"un clapotement sinistre"** -- a sinister splashing sound in her throat (3 March 1881)
- **"les pharmaciens Krishaber"** -- dismissive epithet for her ENT specialist (20 April 1881)
- **"deux jolis imbeciles"** -- two pretty idiots (her last doctors, 9 October 1884)
- **"la malade la plus indisciplinee et la plus imprudente du monde"** -- Potain's description of her, which she read in his intercepted letter (3 July 1884)

## Art and Illness: The Race Against Death

%% GLO_HEALTH.0046 %%
The intersection of Marie's artistic ambition and her declining health is the diary's most poignant thread. She was acutely aware that she was in a race against time, and this awareness intensified both her creative urgency and her despair.

%% GLO_HEALTH.0047 %%
The illness cost her working time directly. She calculated the losses precisely: "De ces deux annees, j'ai ete malade cinq mois, et convalescente ou fievreuse six" (27 November 1882) -- of two years, eleven months lost to disease. She compared her progress to that of Breslau (her chief rival at the Academie Julian), noting: "nous avons marche de pair. Mais alors je tombe malade et perds six ou sept mois" (23 November 1882).

%% GLO_HEALTH.0048 %%
Beyond lost time, the illness affected her work physically. She could not hear her teachers' critiques. She could not hold long painting sessions. By 1884, she could barely stand: "hier j'ai commence a m'habiller pour aller au Bois et j'ai ete deux fois pres d'y renoncer, sans force" (9 October 1884). Yet she continued to paint until the very end, exhibiting at the Salon of 1884 and planning future works even as her body failed.

%% GLO_HEALTH.0049 %%
The race produced one of the diary's most devastating self-assessments: "Lutter et pleurer dix ans; dont six passes a travailler dix heures par jour pour arriver a quoi? Un commencement de talent et une maladie mortelle" (23 March 1884). And yet in the same period she could write with exultant defiance: "je suis encore a un age ou l'on trouve de l'ivresse meme a mourir... j'adore et j'admire tout, tout se presente a moi sous les aspects interessants ou sublimes" (9 March 1884).

%% GLO_HEALTH.0050 %%
## The Body in the Mirror

%% GLO_HEALTH.0051 %%
Marie was intensely attuned to her physical appearance, and the diary tracks her body's transformation with the unflinching eye of a painter. In the early years, she describes herself with narcissistic pleasure -- "des epaules si larges, la poitrine si bombee, les hanches si espagnoles" (28 December 1882). She notes with astonishment that even as tuberculosis is confirmed, her appearance contradicts the diagnosis: "a mon aspect on ne me donnerait jamais, jamais ma maladie, j'ai l'air florissant."

%% GLO_HEALTH.0052 %%
But by 1884, the mirror tells a different story. She watches herself waste: "je maigris, je suis deja maigre" (January 1884); "J'ai maigri de la moitie" (June 1884). The vesicatories and cauterization leave visible marks: "je ne pourrai plus me decolleter pendant quatre mois" -- she cannot wear low-cut dresses, a social impossibility for a young woman of her class. In her penultimate diary entry she notes her "maigreur" and the constant fever. The final mirror image is of a woman who once catalogued her beauty with proprietary pride now watching it dissolve.

%% GLO_HEALTH.0053 %%
Marie's horror of physical decline was not mere vanity. For her, beauty was both social capital and artistic subject. Her self-portraits document the transformation with clinical honesty. The body that was once an instrument of conquest -- of suitors, of social position, of artistic mastery -- becomes a prison, and then a coffin.
