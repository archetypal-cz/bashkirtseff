## Thematic Tag: Emotions

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0002 %%
This thematic tag collects diary paragraphs where Marie explicitly names, analyzes, or dwells on her emotional states -- not simply feeling emotions (every entry does that) but passages where emotion itself becomes the subject of reflection. With 1,228 references across the diary, it is one of the most frequently applied tags, reflecting Marie's extraordinary commitment to mapping her inner life.

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0003 %%
## Overview: Marie as Pioneer of Emotional Self-Analysis

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0004 %%
Marie Bashkirtseff's diary is among the most sustained exercises in emotional self-documentation in 19th-century literature. What sets her apart from other diarists of her era is not the intensity of her feelings -- many Romantic-era writers cultivated intense sensibility -- but her relentless analytical stance toward those feelings. She does not merely feel; she observes herself feeling, names the emotion with precision, questions its authenticity, traces its causes, and judges whether her response is proportionate. This dual posture -- simultaneously experiencing emotion and dissecting it -- anticipates the methods of modern psychological introspection by decades. As she writes on 2 February 1878: "La colere ou le chagrin passe, il faut redevenir comme avant. Le moment ou l'on pleure n'a pas de rapport avec celui qui le suit" (077, 1877-12-26). She articulates a theory of emotional compartmentalization that was far from common in her milieu.

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0005 %%
Her diary was recognized upon its 1887 publication as a "remarkable psychological portrait" and "strikingly modern psychological self-portrait of a young, gifted mind" (Britannica). Writers including Katherine Mansfield, Anais Nin, and Mary MacLane cited it as a model for confessional emotional writing. The concept of [document humain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalism_(literature)) -- authentic human documentation championed by the [Naturalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalism_(literature)) movement -- finds its most vivid personal expression in Marie's emotional inventory.

## Emotional Culture in the 1870s--80s

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0007 %%
### Late Romanticism and the Cult of Feeling

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0008 %%
Marie wrote during the twilight of [Romanticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanticism), when the cult of intense feeling inherited from Goethe's *[Werther](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sorrows_of_Young_Werther)* (1774) and Byron's brooding heroes still shaped emotional culture, even as [Realism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Realism_(arts)) and [Naturalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalism_(literature)) were demanding cooler observation. Marie inhabits both traditions simultaneously. She devours Byron (buying his complete works in October 1874, carnet 024), reads Musset's *Lorenzaccio* and *La Nuit d'Octobre* (carnets 029, 088), and recognizes Werther's lovesickness in Gabriel's behavior: "Des lectures de Goethe, Werther; des figures poetiques et des amertumes" (095, 1882-07-20). Yet she also subjects these Romantic feelings to skeptical analysis -- she is a Romantic who watches herself being Romantic.

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0009 %%
### Gender, Emotion, and the "Nervous Woman"

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0010 %%
In the 1870s--80s, medical authorities considered middle- and upper-class women especially susceptible to [neurasthenia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neurasthenia) -- "nervous exhaustion" -- a diagnosis formalized by George Beard in 1869. Women's "reproductive systems as well as their emotional and intellectual makeup" were thought to make them fragile (Wellcome Collection). Marie uses the period's nervous vocabulary: "attaque de nerfs" (077, 1877-12-26), "je suis nerveuse" (088, 1880-05-24), "les nerfs en corde de violon" (088, 1880-05-24). But she refuses to let "nervousness" explain away her emotions. When she cries, she insists "c'est ne---rveux" with mocking elongation (055, 1876-03-03), simultaneously using and undercutting the medical framework. She recognizes that the label "nervous" was used to dismiss women's legitimate anger and frustration: the tears, the "attaques de nerfs," were often responses to real social constraints, not symptoms of female frailty.

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0011 %%
### Emotional Expression and Social Performance

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0012 %%
Marie was acutely aware that emotional expression in her milieu was governed by strict social codes. Women were permitted -- even expected -- to weep, but rage was unseemly. Her diary records constant tension between felt emotion and performed composure. She cries in private and appears "gaie comme une folle" in public: "Et dire que devant tout le monde a commencer par ma femme de chambre je suis gaie comme une folle, ce n'est pas un masque, c'est une habitude a present" (090, 1880-11-17). The diary becomes the space where the mask drops and the real emotional weather is recorded.

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0013 %%
## Marie's Emotional Vocabulary

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0014 %%
Marie uses a rich, precise vocabulary for her states. Frequency counts across the non-archive original entries give a rough map of her emotional landscape:

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0015 %%
### Negative States (dominant)
- **"triste" / "tristesse"** -- sadness, melancholy. ~86 instances of "je suis triste." The most common emotional declaration in the diary.
- **"malheureuse" / "malheureux"** -- unhappy, wretched. ~281 instances of "je suis heureuse" or "je suis malheureuse" combined, with "malheureuse" far outnumbering "heureuse."
- **"ennui"** -- boredom, existential weariness. Far stronger than English "boredom"; see dedicated section below.
- **"desespoir"** -- despair. Used both casually ("je suis au desespoir" -- merely exasperated) and gravely ("desespoir complet" -- total despair approaching suicidal ideation, 090, 1880-10-05).
- **"furieuse" / "rage" / "colere"** -- anger in graduated intensities. ~73 instances of "je suis furieuse" or "je suis enragee." Marie's anger is often her most articulate emotion.
- **"angoisse"** -- anguish, anxiety. Appears especially in connection with artistic work and health: "c'est chaque jour une angoisse pareille" (100, 1883-07-29).
- **"honte"** -- shame. Both social shame and moral self-judgment: "j'ai honte du resultat et je voudrais tout recommencer" (100, 1883-07-08).
- **"melancolie"** -- melancholy, the lingering, aestheticized Romantic variety. Marie recognizes "le charme souverain de la melancolie" (068, 1876-12-30) -- melancholy as noble, attractive.
- **"degout"** -- disgust, revulsion. Applied to people, situations, and herself.

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0016 %%
### Positive States (rarer, often qualified)
- **"bonheur"** -- happiness. Often hypothetical or fleeting: "bonheur inexprimable" at artistic inspiration (100, 1883-07-21), or the bitter "bonheur parfait n'existe pas" (022, 1874-08-01).
- **"enthousiasme"** -- enthusiasm, excitement. Typically connected to art or social encounters.
- **"joie"** -- joy. Often described as explosive and brief: "La gaiete m'a monte a la tete du coeur, comme la mousse d'une bouteille de champagne" (058, 1876-04-19).
- **"ravie"** -- delighted. Used for artistic breakthroughs and social triumphs.

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0017 %%
### Compound and Complex States
- **"nerveuse"** -- nervous, on edge. A catch-all for emotional states that don't fit neat categories.
- **"marasme"** -- torpor, emotional paralysis. "Je suis dans le marasme" (085, 1879-06-07).
- **"larmes"** -- tears. Not an emotion but Marie's most documented physical response; tears of rage, grief, frustration, joy, and even aesthetic appreciation all receive separate attention.

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0018 %%
## Emotional Patterns

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0019 %%
### The "Emotional Weather Report"

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0020 %%
Marie frequently opens entries with declarative emotional state announcements, functioning as the day's emotional weather forecast. "Je suis triste" (dozens of entries), "Je suis furieuse" (099, 1883-04-23), "Je suis au desespoir" (029, 1875-02-18). In March 1883, she formalizes this pattern into a color-coded system: "Ce n'est pas une journee grise ni blanche, il faut encore un terme. Voyons. Noire, grise, blanche, ce sera comme aujourd'hui et la belle sera rose" (098, 1883-03-30). Black days, grey days, white days, rose days -- she invents a meteorology of mood.

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0021 %%
### Rapid Oscillation

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0022 %%
One of Marie's most distinctive emotional traits is the speed of her mood swings. She documents these with fascinated precision: "Je meurs un instant et dans une seconde je suis gaie, vive et folle, et lorsque je suis au comble de folie je retombe *con maggior forza* dans l'ennui, dans la douleur" (012, 1873-11-10). The musical term *con maggior forza* (with greater force) is telling -- she experiences emotional return as crescendo, each cycle deeper than the last. Her public gaiety coexists with private anguish: "plus je suis malheureuse, plus je suis gaie" (012, 1873-11). The contradiction is not hypocrisy but a coping mechanism she observes with clinical interest.

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0023 %%
### Emotional Triggers

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0024 %%
Marie's emotional states correlate with several recurring triggers:
- **Social isolation** -- the dominant source of suffering in the early carnets. "Depuis trois ans je martelle maman et ma tante" about the family's inadequate social life (029, 1875-02-18).
- **Romantic attachments** -- Hamilton, Antonelli, Pietro, Cassagnac, Bastien-Lepage each generate distinctive emotional registers.
- **Artistic progress or failure** -- later carnets show art replacing romance as the primary emotional driver. "Il n'y a de vrai anxiete et de vrai bonheur que dans les choses de la gloire" (090, 1882-04-23).
- **Health decline** -- hearing loss from 1878 onward adds a layer of constant anxiety and shame.
- **Weather and season** -- "Il pleut, je suis triste" (037, 1875-07); autumn in Nice triggers nostalgia.
- **Music** -- opera and piano produce reliable emotional responses, sometimes more intense than real events.

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0025 %%
### Disproportionate Reactions

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0026 %%
Marie herself notes that her emotional responses are sometimes wildly disproportionate to their triggers. After becoming "very nervous," she cries because there is no kasha at dinner: "il n'y avait pas de kacha et je pleure. Je suis malade" (011, 1873-10-20). She recognizes that these overreactions signal a general emotional state rather than a specific cause -- the tears are about everything, not about kasha. This self-awareness about emotional displacement is remarkably modern.

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0027 %%
## Self-Analysis: Marie on Her Own Emotions

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0028 %%
### The "Two Selves" Theory

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0029 %%
Marie's most sophisticated emotional self-analysis appears in her theory of dual selfhood (077, 1878-02-02). Discussing the horror of loveless marriage, she distinguishes between "la premiere moi" -- the self that feels, desires, and might sin -- and "la seconde, celle qui a soin de la premiere, celle qui desire toutes sortes de choses pour la premiere, celle qui se souvient d'Epictete et qui ne ressent rien pour elle mais a mis tout son bonheur dans la premiere." This "second self" is the observer, the guardian, the one who "remembers Epictetus" -- the analytical faculty that watches the emotional self with protective detachment. This formulation anticipates by decades what psychologists would later call the "observing ego."

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0030 %%
### Questioning Emotional Authenticity

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0031 %%
Marie repeatedly questions whether her emotions are "real" or performed, felt or imagined. About Antonelli: "Me croiriez-vous ? Croyez-vous que je sois amoureuse de ce garcon aux larges narines ? Non n'est-ce pas... Je me suis battue les flancs pour devenir amoureuse, et les cardinaux et le Pape aidant... je me suis exaltee mais d'amour ! Ah ! fi" (088, 1880-05-07). She suspects herself of manufacturing emotions out of boredom, loneliness, or literary influence. In a remarkable passage about imagined feelings, she writes: "il me suffit de m'imaginer une histoire, des evenements, des sentiments pour sentir comme si cela etait arrive au point de rire de bon coeur ou d'etre heureuse ou de verser de vraies larmes" (088, 1880-06-19). The distinction between "real" emotion and vividly imagined emotion fascinates her -- and she is not sure the distinction matters.

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0032 %%
### The Spectator Posture

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0033 %%
Marie cultivates a stance she calls "spectatrice" -- watching herself live and feel as though from an audience. On a romantic evening with Soutzo: "j'assistais en spectatrice voulant voir a quel point on peut etre emue par un homme qu'on n'aime pas" (088, 1880-06-17). Later, about her confused feelings: "a tous ces sentiments confus j'assiste en spectatrice; je m'examine en curieuse" (095, 1882-07-10). This is not emotional coldness but a distinctive form of self-consciousness -- she uses the diary to formalize the gap between experiencing and observing. The act of writing converts raw feeling into analyzed feeling.

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0034 %%
### Emotional Taxonomy

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0035 %%
Marie sometimes attempts systematic classification of emotions. She distinguishes anger from grief by their source: "la colere vous venant de l'exterieur et en s'imposant a vous... Tandis que le chagrin est un sentiment plus intime et que je subis avec plus de resignation" (077, 1877-12-26). She notes that grief, being internal, permits faster recovery: you can laugh five minutes after crying, and this is not heartlessness but "distinguer ses sentiments." She also theorizes about love as a universal "fluide" distributed across all beings (052, 1876-01-14), anticipating Freud's concept of libido by a quarter century.

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0036 %%
## Emotions Across Languages

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0037 %%
Marie's multilingualism provides her with an expanded emotional palette. She switches languages when French cannot adequately express what she feels, and the choice of language itself carries emotional meaning:

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0038 %%
- **Italian** for overwhelming passion and theatrical despair: "O misera me. Ora capisco furore che descrivono nei libri! Capisco tutto cio che credevo varie e gonfiate parole, capisco e provo. O Dio!" (026, 1874-11-19). Italian is the language of opera, and Marie reaches for it when her feelings become operatic.
- **Italian** for musical-emotional terms: "con maggior forza" to describe the crescendo of returning sadness (012, 1873-11-10). Emotional intensity borrows the vocabulary of musical dynamics.
- **English** for emotional distance and contempt: "I feel only pity and contempt" (011, 1873-10-25). English provides coolness when she needs to suppress French/Russian warmth. Also "Broken heart, broken spirits, broken soul" (012, 1873-10) -- English for a catalogue of romantic ruin.
- **Russian** for intimate, untranslatable states: when the register shifts to deeper solemnity, Marie sometimes drops into Russian phrases her French cannot accommodate.

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0039 %%
The code-switching pattern reveals that for Marie, different languages have different emotional temperatures. French is her language of analysis; Italian of passion; English of ironic detachment; Russian of intimate tenderness.

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0040 %%
## Emotions and Art

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0041 %%
From roughly 1877 onward, as Marie becomes serious about painting, the relationship between emotion and artistic work becomes a major theme.

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0042 %%
### Art as Emotional Salvation
- "Il n'y a de vrai anxiete et de vrai bonheur que dans les choses de la gloire" (090, 1882-04-23). Art replaces romance as the primary emotional arena.
- Artistic inspiration produces physical bliss: "C'est une inspiration du ciel et qui me plonge dans un sentiment de bonheur inexprimable" (100, 1883-07-21).
- But artistic failure produces equally physical anguish: "Ces pensees me passent dans la tete comme de gros nuages m'enveloppent d'angoisses qui me donnent froid et me font passer comme des langues de feu sur le corps, dix fois par heure" (090, 1882-04-23).

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0043 %%
### Emotional Transfer to Art
Marie's sculpture explicitly channels her emotional understanding. Her *Nausicaa* figure captures "un abandon si vrai, un desespoir si complet, si jeune, si sincere, si triste que je suis tres empoignee" (098, 1883-03-22). She values sincerity in art above technique: "pour donner une ame a ce limon, il n'y a que Saint Marceaux" (099, 1883-04-18). The emotions she studies in herself become the raw material of her artistic ambition.

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0044 %%
### The Emotional Hierarchy of Art
Marie develops her own aesthetic theory linking emotion to artistic value. She writes that eternal art expresses "des sentiments tels que l'amour, la haine, la douleur. Or ce sont des sentiments eternels" (103, 1884-03-29) -- art that captures timeless emotions transcends period and style. Shakespeare is great because "il n'est ni Anglais, ni aristocrate, ni plebeien et d'aucune epoque. Mais eternellement vrai comme la haine, la douleur, l'amour" (103, 1884-03-29).

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0045 %%
## The Diary as Emotional Technology

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0046 %%
Marie explicitly recognizes her diary as a tool for emotional self-management -- what historians of emotions now call an "emotional technology" (see Scheer, "[Are Emotions a Kind of Practice?](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_emotions)," 2012). As scholarship on 19th-century [diary-writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diary) has shown, the middle classes considered journals "a place where an authentic description of individual emotions should normatively take place" -- and Marie pushes this further than almost any contemporary.

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0047 %%
Her diary serves multiple emotional functions:
- **Catharsis**: "Comme cela soulage d'ecrire ! Je suis plus calme" (004, 1873-05-09). Writing drains emotional pressure.
- **Amplification**: "la bonne idee que j'ai eue d'ecrire mon journal, sans lui je n'avais pas mon paroxysme de douleur de ce soir" (031, 1875-04-06). The diary also *intensifies* feeling -- re-reading old entries triggers fresh emotional crises. Marie is aware the diary is not merely a record but an emotional amplifier.
- **Self-observation**: Writing converts lived emotion into observed emotion. "Tout en me roulant par terre je ne savais pas ce que je pleurais, je pleurais voila tout. Et je trouve un plaisir etrange dans ces larmes" (031, 1875-04-06). The "strange pleasure" comes from the act of analyzing the tears even while shedding them.
- **Mirror**: "Je puis suivre les moindres changements ecrivant devant une glace" (031, 1875-04-06). Marie literally writes in front of a mirror, watching her face change as she writes about emotion -- a physical enactment of the diary's dual function as experience and reflection.
- **Compulsion**: "Ecrire m'est non seulement naturel mais indispensable" (100, 1883-07-14). By 1883, the diary is no longer optional; it is a psychological necessity.

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0048 %%
## Ennui: The Signature Emotion

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0049 %%
If one emotion defines Marie's diary, it is [ennui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ennui) -- a word that encompasses far more than its English near-equivalent "boredom." In 19th-century French culture, ennui was the quintessential malaise of the educated, sensitive soul trapped in a world that failed to match their aspirations. Baudelaire had elevated ennui to cosmic significance in [*Les Fleurs du Mal*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Les_Fleurs_du_mal) (1857), calling it the most dangerous of sins: "Il ferait volontiers de la terre un debris / Et dans un baillement avalerait le monde" (he would gladly reduce the earth to rubble / and in a yawn swallow the world). The related concept of [*spleen*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spleen_(literary)) -- from the organ once thought to produce melancholy -- described a physical malady where ennui suggested a moral one.

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0050 %%
Marie's ennui is pervasive and multifaceted. At fourteen, she already articulates its existential dimension: "Il y a deja deux mois que je ne suis pas moi-meme, quelque chose pese sur moi... je ne puis m'expliquer cet ennui, rien ne m'est arrive et pourtant !" (005, 1873-06-09). The crucial phrase is "rien ne m'est arrive" -- nothing has happened. Her ennui is not caused by circumstances; it is an ontological condition, a feeling of wrongness that exists prior to any particular cause.

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0051 %%
Marie uses "ennui" in multiple registers:
- **Existential ennui**: "Moi aussi je suis triste et je crois que malgre ma peinture, ma sculpture, ma musique, ma litterature, oui malgre tout cela je crois que je m'ennuie" (raw carnet 16). Even achievement fails to cure it.
- **Social ennui**: "O ennui ! Je m'ennuie souvent et tellement que je suis incapable de rien faire" (055, 1876-03-20). The isolation of her family's social position generates crushing boredom.
- **Conversational**: "Ca m'ennuie" (099, 1883-04-30) -- merely "that annoys me," using the word in its quotidian sense.
- **Romantic ennui**: Ennui as the fertile ground from which love grows. "Je subis son influence parce que je suis seule, je m'ennuie, je suis triste" (raw carnet 08) -- she suspects her romantic feelings are products of boredom rather than genuine passion.

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0052 %%
The relationship between ennui and creative drive is central to Marie's psychology. Ennui is both the disease and the goad -- the suffering that makes her want to paint, write, and achieve, and the emptiness that threatens to swallow all achievement. This places her squarely in the tradition of what the [Romantics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanticism) called [*mal du siecle*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mal_du_si%C3%A8cle) -- the sickness of the age, the restless dissatisfaction of a generation that felt itself born too late for heroism and too early for liberation.

## Usage Notes: Tagging Guidelines

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0054 %%
Apply the EMOTIONS tag to paragraphs where Marie:
- **Explicitly names** an emotional state ("je suis triste," "je suis furieuse," "je suis au desespoir")
- **Analyzes** why she feels what she feels, or questions whether the feeling is genuine
- **Theorizes** about emotions in general (the nature of anger vs. grief, the two selves, love as a "fluide")
- **Performs emotional self-observation** ("j'assiste en spectatrice," mirror-writing)
- **Documents rapid mood changes** within a single passage
- **Connects emotions to physical symptoms** (tears, nervous attacks, facial changes)

%% GLO_EMOTIONS.0055 %%
Do **not** tag paragraphs where:
- Emotion is merely present in the narrative (she describes a sad event) without Marie reflecting on her emotional response
- She describes others' emotions without connecting them to her own
- The word "ennui" is used in its casual sense of "annoying" rather than existential boredom
