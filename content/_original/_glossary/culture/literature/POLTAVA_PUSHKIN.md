## Overview

%% GLO_POLTAVA_PUSHKIN.0002 %%
%% [#Pushkin](./PUSHKIN.md) %%
*Poltava* is a narrative poem by [Alexander Pushkin](./PUSHKIN.md), written in 1828 and published in 1829 (ru.wikisource, header of the text). It is about the Ukrainian Cossack hetman Ivan Mazepa and the Battle of Poltava of 1709 between Sweden and Russia. The poem intertwines a love plot between Mazepa and Maria, the daughter of the nobleman Vasily Kochubei, with Mazepa's betrayal of Tsar Peter I and Peter's victory in the battle. It opens with an epigraph from Byron's *Mazeppa* (1819), and it later inspired Tchaikovsky's opera *Mazeppa* of 1884 (Wikipedia, "Poltava (poem)").

## Marie and the poem

%% GLO_POLTAVA_PUSHKIN.0004 %%
%% [#Dikanka](../../places/residences/DIKANKA.md) [#Basile](../../people/historical/BASILE.md) %%
**1 October 1876, Dikanka** (066.0577). Marie is visiting the country house of Prince Serge Kotchoubey, ten kilometres from her father's estate of Gavrontsi, "cette fameuse Dikanka chantée par Pouchkine en même temps que « Les amours de Mazeppa et de Marie Kotchoubey », la dénonciation au tsar et l'exécution du prince Basile Kotchoubey". The diary's footnote adds that Dikanka was celebrated by Pushkin in *Poltava* and is the setting of Gogol's *Evenings on a Farm Near Dikanka*. The "Les amours de Mazeppa et de Marie Kotchoubey" that Marie quotes is her way of naming the love plot of Pushkin's poem; it is not Pushkin's title.

%% GLO_POLTAVA_PUSHKIN.0005 %%
In the same visit (066.0584) she is shown the portrait of Prince Basile (Vasyl Kochubey, 1640-1708, executed by Peter the Great on Mazepa's accusation, so the diary's footnote) and "sa chemise tachée de sang", kept by the family in a cupboard behind the portrait. The poem, in which Kochubey is arrested and executed, is the background of what she sees. Marie "presque se trouve mal".

## Notes and doubts

%% GLO_POLTAVA_PUSHKIN.0007 %%
- The identification of the poem rests on Marie's naming of Pushkin together with Mazeppa and Kotchoubey and on the diary's own footnote. Marie does not say that she has read it, and nothing indicates in which language.
- Whether Pushkin's text itself names Dikanka has not been checked; Marie's phrase is a tourist's cliché of the Kotchoubey estate.
