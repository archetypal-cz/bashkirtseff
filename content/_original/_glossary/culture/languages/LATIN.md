## Overview

This tag marks paragraphs containing Latin-language text within Marie Bashkirtseff's diary. Latin is the fourth most frequently used non-French language, appearing in 233 tagged paragraphs across 209 entries spanning all twelve years of the diary (1873-1884). Unlike Marie's other foreign languages -- English (587 entries), Russian (480), Italian (403) -- Latin is never used for sustained conversation or narrative. Instead, it serves as a reservoir of quotations, mottoes, exclamations, liturgical phrases, and rhetorical ornaments that Marie deploys with striking frequency and considerable flair.

Marie's Latin is the Latin of a brilliantly self-educated young woman who absorbed classical culture through French-language intermediaries -- translations of Plutarch, Homer, and Dante, Catholic liturgy, proverbial wisdom, and the Latinate vocabulary embedded in educated 19th-century French discourse. She does not compose original Latin prose, but she quotes, adapts, invents, misspells, puns on, and personalizes Latin phrases with a confidence that ranges from genuinely learned to cheerfully mock-scholarly. Her signature Latin phrase -- *Gloriae Cupiditas* ("Desire for Glory") -- became her personal motto, inscribed at the beginning and end of nearly every carnet, and recognized by contemporaries as her defining emblem.

## Historical Context: Latin in 19th-Century France

%% GLO_LATIN.0003 %%
### Latin and the French Lycee

[Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) occupied a paradoxical position in 19th-century France: it was simultaneously the foundation of elite male education and a language from which women were systematically excluded.

The French [lycee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyc%C3%A9e) system, established by Napoleon's law of 1808, placed Latin at the absolute center of the secondary curriculum. The syllabus was fixed as "ancient languages, history, rhetoric, logic, music and the elements of mathematical and physical sciences," and Latin composition and translation dominated boys' education from the age of ten through the baccalaureate. Latin was, as educational historian Serge Tomamichel has documented, "the Queen's highway of secondary education" from the birth of the lycee system until the gradual reforms of the late 19th century. For boys of the French bourgeoisie, Latin was not merely a school subject but a social marker -- the ability to quote Virgil, Cicero, or Horace distinguished an educated man from a mere tradesman.

The "Latin question" (*la question du latin*) became a subject of fierce debate in the 1870s and 1880s, precisely during Marie's diary years. Reformers argued that the hours spent on Latin declensions could be better devoted to modern languages, science, and practical skills. Defenders countered that Latin training disciplined the mind and transmitted the heritage of Western civilization. Guy de Maupassant satirized this debate brilliantly in his short story "La Question du latin" (1886). The controversy was part of the broader modernization drive of the [Third French Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Third_Republic), which culminated in the creation of a "modern" (non-classical) secondary track in 1891.

%% GLO_LATIN.0004 %%
### Latin and the Education of Women

The exclusion of women from Latin education was deliberate and ideological. When Republican deputy [Camille See](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camille_S%C3%A9e) drafted the law of December 21, 1880 creating public secondary schools for girls (*lycees de jeunes filles*), he explicitly excluded Latin and Greek from the curriculum. Girls' secondary education was set at five years (versus seven for boys), with no ancient languages and mathematics "not carried to so high a level as in the boys' lycees." The rationale was that women did not need classical training for their social roles as wives and mothers, and that Latin study would make them unwomanly or, worse, intellectual competitors with their husbands.

This exclusion meant that women who knew Latin had acquired it outside the official system -- through private tutors, convent education, self-study, or the kind of ambitious autodidacticism that Marie Bashkirtseff exemplified. Marie, who was "taught entirely by tutors" (AWARE Women Artists, 2024) and devised her own educational program from the age of thirteen, included Latin alongside Italian, English, German, and Greek in her studies. An entry from October 22, 1874 (carnet 024) records: "J'ai repris ma premiere lecon d'italien et de latin" -- she studied Italian and Latin with the same tutor, a common arrangement for Romance-language instruction.

For a young woman in the 1870s to quote Latin fluently -- even imperfectly -- was thus a statement of intellectual ambition. It signaled that she had crossed a gender boundary, claiming access to the cultural capital that the educational system reserved for men.

%% GLO_LATIN.0005 %%
### Latin in Catholic Liturgy

The other major domain of Latin in Marie's world was the [Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church). Throughout the 19th century, the [Tridentine Mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tridentine_Mass) -- promulgated by Pope Pius V in 1570 and unchanged until the reforms of Vatican II in the 1960s -- was celebrated entirely in Latin. Every Catholic in France, from the most devout to the most nominal, heard Latin weekly: the *Kyrie*, *Gloria*, *Credo*, *Sanctus*, *Agnus Dei*, *Pater Noster*, and *Ave Maria* were part of the common cultural vocabulary.

Marie, raised in the Russian Orthodox tradition but deeply embedded in Catholic culture through her years in Nice and Rome, absorbed Catholic Latin naturally. She refers to the *Pater* and *Ave* as childhood prayers (carnet 000), recites the *Pater noster* in moments of crisis (carnet 009), and quotes the *Dies Irae* from memory (carnets 037 and 056). Her encounter with [Pope Pius IX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Pius_IX) in Rome produced a signed photograph inscribed "*Pax vobis*" (Peace be with you) -- a papal formula she treasured and displayed (carnet 055, March 26, 1876). She invokes *Requiescat in pace* with ironic detachment (carnet 056, April 2, 1876) and exclaims *Miserere!* -- the opening word of Psalm 51 -- as a habitual cry of distress throughout the diary.

%% GLO_LATIN.0006 %%
### The Latin Quarter and Academic Culture

Paris's [Latin Quarter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_Quarter,_Paris) (*Quartier Latin*), where Marie lived and studied from 1877, took its name from the medieval tradition of Latin-language instruction at the [University of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris) and the [Sorbonne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris#The_Sorbonne) (founded 1257). Students and professors had spoken Latin in the university precinct until the French Revolution. By Marie's time, the neighborhood retained its association with intellectual life, bohemian culture, and artistic ambition -- though Latin was no longer heard in its cafes. The [Academie Julian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acad%C3%A9mie_Julian), where Marie studied painting from 1877, was located in this quarter.

Latin also pervaded the world of academic and professional discourse. Legal terminology (*habeas corpus*, *de facto*, *de jure*), medical vocabulary, scientific nomenclature, and philosophical concepts were all expressed in Latin. Mottoes of institutions, families, and nations drew on Latin. An educated person in the 1870s encountered Latin daily -- on buildings, in courtrooms, in church, in newspapers, and in the speech of fellow intellectuals.

## Marie's Latin: Education and Proficiency

%% GLO_LATIN.0008 %%
### How Marie Learned Latin

Marie's Latin education was part of her extraordinary program of self-directed study. At approximately age thirteen, she devised a plan for her own education that included Latin among Italian, English, German, and Greek. She studied Latin formally with a tutor -- the same tutor who taught her Italian, as recorded in her October 22, 1874 entry -- and her knowledge was supplemented by her voracious reading of French translations of classical authors.

She read [Plutarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutarch) (carnet 038, August 3, 1875: "Je vais lire Plutarque et puis prier Dieu"), [Homer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer) (the *Odyssey*, discussed in carnet 024), [Plato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato), [Dante](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dante_Alighieri), and [Shakespeare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Shakespeare) -- choosing these authors for travel reading in a scene that reveals her intellectual ambitions (carnet 062, July 3, 1876). She was familiar with Balzac's use of Latin tags and classical allusions. She knew the *Dies Irae* well enough to quote multiple stanzas from memory, and she recognized the Publius Syrus maxim "Est socia mortis homini vita ingloria" as articulating her own existential philosophy.

Her Latin, however, was not that of a trained classicist. She made grammatical errors -- using "cupiditate" (ablative) and "cupiditas" (nominative) interchangeably in her motto, and once writing "Gloriae Cupididate" with a misspelling (carnet 053, January 26, 1876). She invented mock-Latin phrases like "Requiem delectabile" (carnet 038, August 13, 1875), which her own annotator marked "[sic]" as garbled or invented. She confused cases and occasionally produced Latin that a classicist would wince at. But this imperfect fluency was itself characteristic -- Marie's Latin was not academic performance but personal expression, and she wielded it with the same confident disregard for convention that marked her French prose.

%% GLO_LATIN.0009 %%
### Marie's Self-Assessment

Marie was characteristically self-aware about the gap between her Latin posturing and genuine scholarship. In an entry from May 20, 1880 (carnet 088), she describes making a pun on *fiat lux* ("let there be light," from [Genesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Genesis) 1:3) and *luxe* (luxury): "Et qui entendant parler du luxe des jeunes filles fait l'infame calembour de fiat lux." She immediately undercuts herself: "Si encore j'etais reellement savante mais je ne suis que poseuse !" ("If I were actually learned -- but I'm only a show-off!"). This confession -- that her Latin is more display than depth -- is itself a display of the disarming honesty that makes her diary unique.

The sculptor [Carrier-Belleuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert-Ernest_Carrier-Belleuse), meeting Marie at a reception in January 1882, was struck by her Latin motto: he was "tout ramolli et sentimental en insistant sur la cruaute de la devise Gloriae Cupiditas" ("all softened and sentimental, insisting on the cruelty of the motto Gloriae Cupiditas") (carnet 094, January 13, 1882). The motto had become her public identity -- an artistic persona expressed in a dead language.

## Gloriae Cupiditas: Marie's Motto

%% GLO_LATIN.0011 %%
### Origin and Adoption

The phrase *Gloriae Cupiditas* (or *Gloriae Cupiditate* in the ablative) -- "Desire for Glory" -- is the single most important Latin expression in the diary. It appears in dozens of entries across nearly every year, inscribed as the title or epigraph of individual carnets, and became Marie's recognized personal motto.

The classical Latin phrase *cupiditas gloriae* appears in [Cicero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cicero) and [Tacitus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tacitus), where it carries ambivalent moral weight -- glory-seeking is both the engine of great deeds and a dangerous form of vanity. The Perseus Digital Library records the classical usage: "to be consumed by the fires of ambition: gloriae, laudis cupiditate incensum esse, flagrare." Marie adopted this phrase and made it entirely her own.

The motto first appears in January 1876 (carnet 053), when Marie declares she must change her previous motto: "Ma devise ne devrait pas etre Audacer et amanter, mais bien Gloriae cupiditate. Je la changerai" ("My motto should not be *Audacer et amanter* [Boldly and lovingly], but rather *Gloriae cupiditate*. I will change it") (January 25, 1876). The earlier motto -- *Audacer et amanter* -- reflects the romantic Marie of the early carnets, infatuated with the Duke of Hamilton. The new motto marks her pivot toward artistic ambition and the pursuit of fame.

Two days later, she articulates the motto's significance with remarkable clarity: "La seule chose qui soit dans mon fond c'est le Gloriae Cupiditate. Tout le reste m'occupe beaucoup certainement, mais tout le reste est du domaine de la surface" ("The only thing that is truly at my core is the Gloriae Cupiditate. Everything else occupies me greatly, certainly, but everything else belongs to the surface") (carnet 053, January 27, 1876).

%% GLO_LATIN.0012 %%
### Variants and Evolution

Marie's Latin was not always consistent. The motto appears in several grammatical forms across the diary:

- **Gloriae Cupiditas** (nominative) -- "Desire for Glory" -- the most common form, used as carnet titles
- **Gloriae Cupiditate** (ablative) -- "From/With desire for glory" -- used in running text and early carnets
- **Gloriae Cupididate** -- a misspelling (carnet 053, January 26, 1876)
- **Glorias Cupiditate** -- another variant (raw carnet 06)
- **Gloria Cupditas** -- garbled (raw carnet 16)
- **Gloriae Cupididas** -- another misspelling (raw carnet 11)
- **Cupiditas gloriae** -- inverted word order (carnet 087, January 13, 1880)

The inconsistency reveals that Marie was writing from memory and feeling rather than from grammatical precision. She sometimes combined the motto with other Latin: "Volo omnia gloriae cupiditate solum modo habeo !" ("I want everything -- [but] I only have desire for glory!") (raw carnet 06) -- a phrase that captures her characteristic oscillation between limitless ambition and frustrated reality.

In the early carnets (up to approximately 1877), the motto was often paired with the coded initials "H.G.t.D.o.H." -- "His Grace the Duke of Hamilton" -- showing how her ambition and romantic longing were intertwined: "*Gloriae cupiditate* [His] Grace] t[he] D[uke] of[f] H[amilton]" (carnet 063, July 19, 1876). By the later carnets, when Marie had committed herself to painting, the Hamilton cipher disappears and *Gloriae Cupiditas* stands alone -- ambition purified of romantic attachment.

%% GLO_LATIN.0013 %%
### Cultural Significance of the Motto

For a young woman to adopt a Latin motto in the 1870s was itself a provocation. Latin belonged to male institutional culture -- family coats of arms, military regiments, universities, and the Church. Marie's claim to a personal Latin motto asserted her right to the same kind of public ambition that men signified through classical language.

The motto also connected her to the tradition of the [Stoics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoicism) and Roman moralists, for whom *gloria* was a legitimate aspiration tempered by duty and virtue. Marie's version stripped away the Stoic moderation: her desire for glory was unashamed, absolute, and frequently self-mocking. When Carrier-Belleuse called the motto "cruel," Marie's associate Dina showed him her paintings to demonstrate that the ambition was backed by talent: "bien que je les eusse cachees Dina a montre certaines etudes a qui de droit" (carnet 094).

### Dies Irae Quotations

Marie quotes the medieval [*Dies Irae*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dies_irae) hymn -- the sequence from the Catholic [Requiem Mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Requiem) -- at length on two separate occasions, copying out multiple stanzas with accent marks that suggest she was working from a printed source (a missal or hymnal):

**Carnet 037** (July 31, 1875): "lacrimosa dies illa. Qua resurget ex favilla" / "Judicandus homo reus... mie [sic, for *pie*] ergo parce, Deus" -- accompanied by a profile drawing and rows of chevrons, as if she were doodling during a moment of religious reflection.

**Carnet 056** (March 28, 1876): "Lacrymosa dies illa / Qua resurget ex favilla / Judicandus homo reus / Huic ergo parce Deus" -- a cleaner transcription spread across four separate paragraphs, suggesting more deliberate copying.

The *Dies Irae* ("Day of Wrath") is attributed to the 13th-century Franciscan [Thomas of Celano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_of_Celano) and was a standard part of the Requiem Mass until 1970. Its themes of judgment, mortality, and divine mercy resonated with Marie's persistent preoccupation with death and the afterlife.

## Patterns and Functions of Marie's Latin

%% GLO_LATIN.0022 %%
### Distribution Over Time

Latin usage is not evenly distributed across the diary. Analysis of tagged paragraphs by year reveals a clear pattern:

- **1873**: 11 entries -- scattered exclamations (*Miserere!*, *Pater noster*)
- **1874**: 41 entries -- peak of Latin study; motto development begins
- **1875**: 39 entries -- *Dies Irae* quotations; *Veni, vidi, vici*; abundant *Miserere!*
- **1876**: 52 entries -- highest frequency; motto fully adopted; Roman period; classical quotations
- **1877**: 15 entries -- *Sic transit gloria ducis*; *Audaces fortuna juvat*
- **1878**: 13 entries -- *Gloriae Cupiditas* as carnet title
- **1879-1884**: 37 entries combined -- declining frequency; Latin now primarily limited to the motto and occasional phrases

The peak in 1874-1876 corresponds to Marie's period of most intense self-education and her extended stay in Rome, where Latin was omnipresent in inscriptions, churches, and cultural life. The decline after 1877 reflects her shift from academic study to full-time painting at the Academie Julian, where classical languages were irrelevant.

%% GLO_LATIN.0023 %%
### Functional Categories

Marie's Latin serves five distinct functions:

1. **Identity marker**: The *Gloriae Cupiditas* motto defines her public and private persona. It is not decoration but self-definition -- "la seule chose qui soit dans mon fond."

2. **Emotional intensifier**: *Miserere!* is her most frequent Latin exclamation, appearing across the entire diary span. She uses it for everything from genuine anguish ("Oh! triple Miserere!" -- carnet 015, January 7, 1874) to mild theatrical annoyance ("Miserere ! Je vais lire Plutarque" -- carnet 038, August 3, 1875). The Latin elevates quotidian frustration to dramatic register.

3. **Intellectual display**: Quoting Publius Syrus, Virgil, or the *Dies Irae* signals Marie's classical learning in a culture that denied women access to Latin education. The display is sometimes self-conscious ("je ne suis que poseuse!"), but the knowledge is real.

4. **Wordplay and wit**: Marie's *Sic transit gloria ducis* -- "Thus passes the glory of the duke" -- is a brilliant pun on the proverbial *sic transit gloria mundi*, repurposed to mourn the Duke of Hamilton's fading importance in her life (carnet 075, October 6, 1877). Her *fiat lux* / *luxe* pun and her invented *Turpis rana* / *Turpissima filia* insults show Latin as a playground for her verbal inventiveness.

5. **Liturgical resonance**: The Catholic Latin phrases (*Pax vobis*, *Requiescat in pace*, *Dies Irae*, *Miserere*) connect Marie to the religious world she simultaneously inhabits and questions. She uses liturgical Latin with a range that spans genuine devotion, ironic detachment, and theatrical self-dramatization.

%% GLO_LATIN.0024 %%
### Latin vs. Marie's Other Languages

Compared to her use of English, Russian, Italian, and German, Marie's Latin is distinctive in several ways:

- **Never conversational**: Marie never uses Latin for dialogue, narration, or extended communication. Every other language in her repertoire serves practical communication; Latin is purely rhetorical.
- **Never private**: While Russian signals family intimacy and English signals romantic longing, Latin signals public ambition and intellectual identity. It is the language of display, not concealment.
- **Most allusive**: Latin phrases almost always carry an intertextual reference -- to the Bible, classical literature, Catholic liturgy, or proverbial wisdom. Marie's English and Italian are spontaneous; her Latin is citational.
- **Most imperfect**: Her English, Italian, and Russian are all fluent to varying degrees. Her Latin contains the most errors -- misspellings, garbled phrases, grammatical inconsistencies -- suggesting it was learned primarily through reading and memorization rather than systematic grammar study.

## Usage Notes for Tagging

- If a paragraph is entirely in Latin (e.g., the *Gloriae Cupiditas* motto standing alone), tag only with #Latin

- The *Dies Irae* quotations and other liturgical Latin should be tagged #Latin even when they appear in a religious context that might suggest tagging only #Religion

## Usage Notes for Translators

Latin code-switching in Marie's diary requires different treatment depending on function:

- **Mottoes** (*Gloriae Cupiditas*, *Audacer et amanter*): Preserve in Latin in all target languages. These are proper names of Marie's self-concept, not translatable phrases. Provide translation in a footnote on first occurrence.
- **Liturgical phrases** (*Pater noster*, *Ave Maria*, *Miserere*, *Pax vobis*, *Dies Irae*): Preserve in Latin. These are universally recognized Catholic terminology that would lose their character in translation. Footnote only if the target audience may be unfamiliar with Catholic liturgy.
- **Classical quotations** (*Audaces fortuna juvat*, *Veni, vidi, vici*, *Vanitas vanitatum*): Preserve in Latin with translation in footnote. These are part of a shared European cultural heritage.
- **Conversational Latin** (*nec plus ultra*, *in extremis*, *in petto*, *propria persona*, *vice versa*): Handle according to the target language's conventions. If the phrase exists as a standard borrowing in the target language (e.g., Czech uses *vice versa*), translate naturally. If not, preserve in Latin with gloss.
- **Playful/invented Latin** (*Turpis rana*, *Requiem delectabile*): Preserve in Latin to maintain the mock-scholarly effect. Marie's humor depends on the incongruity of Latin in everyday contexts.
- **Wordplay** (*Sic transit gloria ducis*, *fiat lux / luxe*): Preserve the Latin and explain the pun in a footnote. These are untranslatable as wordplay but essential to Marie's character.
