## Overview

This tag marks paragraphs containing Italian-language text within the diary. Italian is the third most frequently used foreign language in Marie Bashkirtseff's diary after French (the primary language) and English. With 398 tagged paragraphs, Italian appears in contexts spanning opera titles and quotations, Roman Carnival conversations, art terminology, emotional exclamations, and interactions with Italian acquaintances -- particularly during Marie's two extended stays in Rome (1876 and 1876-1877).

Marie began formal Italian lessons on March 17, 1872, in Nice, studying initially with a teacher called "la Micaletti" (carnet 001, February 12, 1873 entry). By September 1873, she had progressed to conversational ability under her teacher Michelezzi: "J'ai pour la premiere fois parle l'italien aujourd'hui. Le pauvre Michelezzi faillit tomber evanoui ou se jeter par la fenetre" ("I spoke Italian for the first time today. Poor Michelezzi nearly fainted or threw himself out the window") (carnet 009, September 15, 1873). She proudly counted Italian among her four spoken languages (alongside Russian, French, and English), while also studying German and Latin.

## Historical Context: Italy in Marie's Era

%% GLO_ITALIAN.0002 %%

The Italy of Marie's lifetime was a dramatically new nation. The [Italian unification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unification_of_Italy) (*Risorgimento*) had culminated only three years before Marie's diary begins: Rome was captured from the Papal States on September 20, 1870, and declared capital of the [Kingdom of Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Italy) on February 3, 1871. When Marie first visited Rome in 1876, the city had been the national capital for barely five years. The transformation from papal theocracy to secular monarchy was still raw and visible.

Marie herself observed this transition with remarkable political sophistication. In an April 1876 entry (carnet 057), she wrote a lengthy analysis of the consequences of unification for the Roman aristocracy:

> "Rome sans le Pape etait tout a fait une ville a part et souveraine du monde dans son genre. Alors, chaque prince romain etait comme un petit roi, il avait sa cour et ses clients comme dans l'Antiquite. [...] Certes dans deux generations il n'y aura plus ni grandeur, ni richesse, car Rome est soumise aux lois royales et Rome deviendra comme Naples, Milan et les autres villes de l'Italie."

She noted that before annexation, Count Pietro Antonelli's uncle (the powerful [Cardinal Giacomo Antonelli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giacomo_Antonelli), Secretary of State to Pius IX) held enormous influence, but "a present sa position est assez embarrassee" (carnet 055, March 18, 1876).

Key figures of the new Italy appear in the diary: the [House of Savoy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Savoy) royal family, whose [Quirinal Palace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quirinal_Palace) replaced the Pope's residence as the seat of power; Count Mirafiore, the morganatic son of [Victor Emmanuel II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Emmanuel_II); and the great Roman princely families (Torlonia, Doria, Borghese, Ruspoli, Schiarra) whose fortunes Marie predicted would decline under the new regime.

## Nice's Italian Heritage

%% GLO_ITALIAN.0003 %%

Nice, where Marie spent much of her childhood and adolescence, had been part of Italy -- specifically the [Kingdom of Sardinia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Sardinia) -- until 1860, just thirteen years before Marie's diary begins. The [County of Nice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_of_Nice) (*Contea di Nizza*) had been under [Savoyard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Savoy) rule since 1388. It was ceded to France by the [Treaty of Turin (1860)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treaty_of_Turin_(1860)) as compensation for French military support during the [Second Italian War of Independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Italian_War_of_Independence).

The annexation was deeply contested. A quarter of Nice's population -- some 11,000 people -- chose voluntary exile in Italy rather than accept French rule, a movement associated with [Italian irredentism in Nice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_irredentism_in_Nice). The historical language of the region, [Nicois/Nicart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ni%C3%A7ard_dialect) (*Nissart*), had characteristics of both Occitan and [Ligurian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ligurian_(Romance_language)) (a Gallo-Italian language), and before 1860 all official archives were written in Italian or Ligurian. The French government pursued active *francisation* after annexation, but Italian cultural and linguistic influence remained strong throughout Marie's years there (1870s).

This Italian heritage was visible in the cultural institutions Marie frequented. Nice had a dedicated **Theatre Italien** alongside the French theater, and Italian opera dominated the performance calendar. Marie attended performances at both venues throughout 1873, hearing Verdi's *Un ballo in maschera* and *La Forza del destino*, Bellini's *La Sonnambula*, Donizetti's *Lucia di Lammermoor* and *La Favorita*, and other Italian operas. Many performers, including the prima donna Galletti and the soprano Scarati, were Italian. The streets, architecture, and daily life of Nice still bore the imprint of nearly five centuries of Italian governance.

## Marie's Italian Proficiency

%% GLO_ITALIAN.0004 %%

Marie's Italian education followed the trajectory typical of the Russian aristocracy abroad, moving from formal instruction to practical fluency through immersion:

**1872 -- Formal studies begin**: Italian lessons started on March 17, 1872, with "la Micaletti" in Nice (carnet 001, February 12, 1873). The initial instruction likely focused on reading, grammar, and written exercises, as was standard in 19th-century language pedagogy.

**1873 -- First spoken Italian**: By September 15, 1873, Marie could speak Italian conversationally, to the astonishment of her teacher Michelezzi (carnet 009). She also studied Latin alongside Italian, recognizing the connection between the two (carnet 024, October 22, 1874: "J'ai repris ma premiere lecon d'italien et de latin"). She wrote Italian letters (carnet 011, October 26, 1873: "J'ai ecrit une lettre italienne pour Georges").

**1874 -- Growing confidence**: By autumn 1874, Marie was confident enough to spar linguistically with Italian-speaking acquaintances, though she acknowledged limitations in fluency: "je sais bien assez cette langue pour ne pas le devenir [confuse], une seule chose me manque, c'est to speak fluently" (carnet 024, September 16, 1874).

**1876 -- Full conversational command**: During the Roman Carnival (February-March 1876, carnets 054-055), Marie used Italian extensively as a practical communication tool and social weapon. She spoke Italian to disguise herself from Russian acquaintances: "je fis faire cercle autour de moi et parlai italien, et ils s'en allerent disant l'un a l'autre qu'ils etaient betes et que j'etais une Italienne" (carnet 054, February 18, 1876). She conversed with Count Bruschetti in Italian on philosophical topics (carnet 055, March 22, 1876), flirted with Pietro Antonelli in Italian at masked balls while disguising her voice (carnet 054, February 29, 1876), and even forbade Antonelli from speaking Italian to increase his awkwardness: "je lui defendais de parler italien et parler une langue etrangere c'est toujours genant a un certain point" (carnet 055, March 16, 1876). Her Italian was fluent enough to receive compliments from native speakers "sur la facon dont je parle l'italien" (carnet 054, February 23, 1876).

Marie's command of Italian was thus not merely academic but genuinely practical -- she could pass for Italian among strangers, conduct philosophical conversations, write letters, and navigate daily life in Rome.

## Italian in Art and Music

%% GLO_ITALIAN.0005 %%

### Opera

Italian opera was the dominant musical art form of Marie's world, and her diary is saturated with Italian operatic references. The operas she attended or mentions include:

- **Giuseppe Verdi** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giuseppe_Verdi)): *Un ballo in maschera* (carnet 001), *La Forza del destino* -- whose French premiere took place at Nice (carnet 002), *Aida* (carnet 068 -- "Je chante Aida. La musique m'enivre, m'enamoure, m'affole"), *Rigoletto* (carnet 068), *La Traviata* (carnet 068), *Don Carlos* (carnet 068)
- **Vincenzo Bellini** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincenzo_Bellini)): *La Sonnambula* (carnet 001), *Norma*
- **Gaetano Donizetti** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaetano_Donizetti)): *Lucia di Lammermoor* (carnet 002), *La Favorita* (carnets 004, 027), *Lucrezia Borgia* (carnets 003, 007)
- **Gaspare Spontini**: *La Vestale* (carnet 055 -- Pietro Antonelli recalls falling in love with Marie at a performance)

The [Opera de Nice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Op%C3%A9ra_de_Nice), built in 1826 in the Italian style with horseshoe-shaped boxes, was designed during Nice's Sardinian period and naturally featured Italian-language performances. The opera house was destroyed by fire on March 23, 1881 (during a performance of *Lucia di Lammermoor*), and reconstructed under architect Francois Aune; it reopened on February 7, 1885 with Verdi's *Aida*.

Marie's relationship with Italian opera was deeply personal. She had aspired to a singing career before losing her voice to tuberculosis around 1877. Italian was the essential language of vocal music: the [bel canto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bel_canto) tradition, vocal terminology (*prima donna*, *maestro*, *roulades*), and the emotional vocabulary of song were all Italian. In Rome, she sang *Aida* alone in empty rooms and at the piano (carnet 068, December 30-31, 1876), and performed Italian songs like "Santa Lucia" for the Howard children in Nice (carnet 002, March 9, 1873).

### Visual Art

Italian was equally the language of art. Marie took painting lessons with [Wilhelm Kotarbinski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Kotarbi%C5%84ski) in Rome, discovering her passion for visual art. Italian art terminology permeated the vocabulary of European painters: *studio*, *atelier* (from Italian *bottega* tradition), *chiaroscuro*, *sfumato*, *fresco*, *impasto*. Marie engaged intensely with Italian masters -- she admired Michelangelo above all ("Michel-Ange meme Buonorotti, c'est grand comme Dieu" -- carnet 100, August 5, 1883), challenged the cult of Raphael ("Raphael me fatigue" -- same entry), and visited Italian galleries and the envois de Rome (works sent from the [French Academy in Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Academy_in_Rome) at the [Villa Medici](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villa_Medici)).

The French Academy in Rome, housed in the Villa Medici since 1803, sent winners of the [Prix de Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prix_de_Rome) to study Italian masters. Although Marie studied at the [Academie Julian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acad%C3%A9mie_Julian) in Paris rather than the Villa Medici (which was closed to women until the 20th century), the Italian art world formed the backdrop and aspiration of her training.

## Code-Switching Patterns

%% GLO_ITALIAN.0006 %%

Marie's use of Italian in the diary follows several distinct patterns:

1. **Opera titles and quotations**: The most frequent use. Opera titles appear in Italian (*Un ballo in maschera*, *La Forza del destino*, *Lucrezia Borgia*, *Aida*, etc.) as do direct quotations from libretti and Italian songs. Example: "La bocca mi bacia tutta tremante" (from Dante's *Inferno*, Canto V, quoted at the Vienna Exposition -- carnet 007, August 4, 1873).

2. **Conversation in Rome**: During her Roman stays, Marie reports extensive Italian-language conversations with Italian acquaintances -- Antonelli, Bruschetti, Torlonia, and others. She sometimes transcribes their words in Italian: "Tu sais, dit-il toujours en italien, nous devrions nous marier" ("You know, he said, still in Italian, we should get married" -- carnet 054, February 29, 1876).

3. **Disguise and performance**: Marie used Italian strategically to conceal her identity during the Roman Carnival, fooling Russians into thinking she was Italian (carnet 054, February 18, 1876). She also used it to amuse and confuse -- speaking Italian and English to bewildered Germans on a train (carnet 007, August 1, 1873).

4. **Italian place names and cultural terms**: Roman topography appears in Italian: *Corso*, *Pincio*, *Villa Borghese*, *Piazza del Popolo*, *Via del Babuino*, *Quirinale*, *Campidoglio*. Italian social terms include *fumatori* (smoking compartment), *tombola*, *cardinalino* (little cardinal -- her nickname for Antonelli).

5. **Emotional and superlative expressions**: Phrases expressing intensity: "a piu non posso" (as much as possible), "cosa rarissima" (a very rare thing), "buia compagna" (dark companion -- literary).

6. **Wordplay and puns**: The silver heart rings engraved "A.T.E." -- simultaneously the initials of the three Graces (Aglae, Thalie, Euphrosine) and Italian "a te" ("to you"), plus A for Antonelli and T for Torlonia (carnet 056, April 3, 1876).

7. **Art criticism vocabulary**: Italian terms for discussing painting and sculpture, Italian proper names for artworks and artists (Michelangelo, Raffaello Sanzio, Benvenuto Cellini, Dante).

## Italy's Cultural World in the 1870s-80s

%% GLO_ITALIAN.0008 %%

### Rome as Artistic Capital

Rome remained the supreme destination for European artists throughout the 19th century. The [French Academy in Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Academy_in_Rome), established in 1666 and housed at the [Villa Medici](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villa_Medici) since 1803, sent the finest young French artists (winners of the [Prix de Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prix_de_Rome)) to study classical and Renaissance masterpieces. The annual *envois de Rome* -- works produced by Academy fellows and exhibited in Paris -- were a major event in the French art calendar. Marie viewed these exhibitions and commented on them critically (carnet 100, June 24, 1883).

An international colony of artists, writers, and musicians gathered in Rome. Marie encountered Polish painter [Wilhelm Kotarbinski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Kotarbi%C5%84ski), Russian sculptor Popoff, and many others in the cosmopolitan artistic milieu. The great private collections of the Roman aristocracy -- the Borghese, Doria Pamphili, and Torlonia galleries -- were accessible to visitors of sufficient social standing, and Marie visited several.

### Italian Opera

The period of Marie's diary (1873-1884) spans a pivotal moment in Italian operatic history. [Giuseppe Verdi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giuseppe_Verdi) (1813-1901) dominated the international stage. His *Aida* (1871) had recently premiered; *Otello* (1887) and *Falstaff* (1893) lay ahead. The [bel canto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bel_canto) tradition of [Bellini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincenzo_Bellini) (1801-1835) and [Donizetti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaetano_Donizetti) (1797-1848) remained popular in the repertoire, though their florid vocal style had been superseded by Verdi's more dramatic approach. Italian opera was performed across Europe in Italian, making the language essential cultural currency for the upper classes.

### The Kingdom of Italy

The [Kingdom of Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Italy) (1861-1946), unified under the [House of Savoy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Savoy), was in Marie's day a young and still-consolidating nation. The [capture of Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capture_of_Rome) in 1870 from the Papal States completed territorial unification but created the "[Roman Question](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Question)" -- the Pope's refusal to recognize the Italian state, which would persist until 1929. Marie witnessed this tension firsthand in the divided loyalties of Roman society: the papal ("Black") aristocracy (like the Antonelli family) versus the royal ("White") aristocracy that supported the Savoy monarchy.

### Italian Literature

Marie was familiar with the Italian literary canon. She quotes [Dante](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dante_Alighieri) directly (the *Inferno*'s Francesca da Rimini episode at the Vienna Exposition; a metaphor from *Inferno* about flowers beaten by frost, applied to herself -- carnet 068, February 22-23, 1877). She references [Benvenuto Cellini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benvenuto_Cellini)'s autobiography when comparing his artistic frenzy to her own (carnet 100, July 11, 1883). She also read [Stendhal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stendhal)'s writings on Italian art and culture, including his assessments of Raphael.

## Translator Notes

%% GLO_ITALIAN.0010 %%

Italian code-switching in Marie's diary serves multiple functions that translators should preserve:

1. **Cultural marker**: Italian signals Marie's education, cosmopolitanism, and social milieu. Preserve Italian phrases in translation; do not translate them into the target language. Provide footnotes for phrases that would be opaque to modern readers.

2. **Emotional register**: Marie's switches to Italian often mark heightened emotional states -- passion ("Je chante Aida. La musique m'enivre, m'enamoure, m'affole"), romantic intrigue (Carnival conversations with Antonelli), or intellectual display (philosophical conversation with Bruschetti).

3. **Social strategy**: Marie deliberately uses Italian to deceive, flirt, or assert dominance. Translators should make clear when Italian is being used strategically rather than incidentally.

4. **Opera context**: Italian opera titles and quotations should remain in Italian with appropriate annotation. The emotional significance of specific operas to Marie's narrative should be noted -- *Un ballo in maschera* (masked balls, forbidden love) at the very start of the diary sets a thematic tone.

5. **Nice's bilingual context**: In the Nice entries, Italian is ambient -- the theater is called the "Theatre Italien," performers have Italian names, Italian culture is the air Marie breathes. This is not exotic code-switching but the natural linguistic environment of a city that was Italian thirteen years earlier.

6. **Period considerations**: Marie's Italian reflects 19th-century educated usage. Some phrases she uses (e.g., "a piu non posso") are standard but somewhat literary. Translators should preserve this register.
