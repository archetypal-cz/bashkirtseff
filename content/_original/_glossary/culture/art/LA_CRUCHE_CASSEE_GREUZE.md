## Overview

%% GLO_LA_CRUCHE_CASSEE_GREUZE.0002 %%
*La Cruche cassée* (The Broken Pitcher) is the best-known painting of Jean-Baptiste [Greuze](../history/GREUZE.md) (1725-1805), in the Louvre (inventory INV 5036). It is oil on canvas, about 108.5 × 86.5 cm, of a young girl in a loosened bodice, with flowers gathered in her apron and a broken pitcher on her arm, her expression a mixture of dismay and innocence. The pitcher and the flowers are the traditional emblems of lost innocence (the reader's note to 094.0167 gives the same reading). Sources differ on the date: the Louvre-linked notice gives 1772-1773, the Petit Palais catalogue 1771-1772, and the picture was first exhibited at the Salon of 1777 (Christie's catalogue note).

%% GLO_LA_CRUCHE_CASSEE_GREUZE.0003 %%
The picture was a staple of nineteenth-century reproductions and of the taste for the delicate, sentimental eighteenth century that Marie also shows in her admiration of Watteau and Greuze (see the [Greuze](../history/GREUZE.md) entry).

## Marie and this painting

%% GLO_LA_CRUCHE_CASSEE_GREUZE.0005 %%
Marie does not discuss the painting; she uses it twice as a pattern for her own appearance, a girl as "innocence" in costume.

%% GLO_LA_CRUCHE_CASSEE_GREUZE.0006 %%
On **30 December 1879** she is in Paris, going to the Théâtre-Français: «Je m'arrange en cruche cassée de Greuze, ce qui me va bien malgré tous ces tourments des derniers jours.» Gabriel, who dines out, arrives late and sees them only at the exit; she says she always gets what she wants and, in her words, «je pose pour la femme heureuse» (087.0101).

%% GLO_LA_CRUCHE_CASSEE_GREUZE.0007 %%
On **13 January 1882**, at a ball, she wears a gown made by her and the couturier Doucet, «la reproduction presque fidèle de la Cruche cassée de Greuze»: hair twisted into a small chignon on the nape, a great cord of Bengal roses losing itself in a pleated short skirt of silk muslin, a laced satin bodice with a kerchief tied askew, a second skirt looped up into panniers, one filled with roses (094.0167). A footnote to the paragraph records the Louvre painting. She adds that she looked charming (and that an odious young doctor tried to stop her dancing).

Two days later, on **15 January 1882**, a long newspaper article on the evening (by a woman writer, apparently the columnist the family had just visited, [Étincelle](../../people/writers/ETINCELLE.md)) compares Marie to the *Cruche cassée*. The family is not pleased and fears that in Poltava the comparison will pass for an insult. Two days before, the same writer had called Marie one of the prettiest women of the Russian Empire; this time she merely describes the dress (094.0177).
