# Nice

%% GLO_NICE.0001 %%
**Research Status**: Comprehensive
**Last Updated**: 2026-05-24
**Diary Coverage**: Throughout diary (1873-1884), most intensely 1873-1877; returns periodically thereafter
**References**: 827+ across all carnets (13th most-referenced glossary entry)

## Overview

%% GLO_NICE.0002 %%
Nice (Occitan: *Nissa*; Italian: *Nizza*) was Marie Bashkirtseff's primary home during the formative years of her diary (1873-1877) and the city where she began writing on January 11, 1873. A fashionable winter resort on the French Riviera, Nice had been annexed from the Kingdom of Sardinia only thirteen years before Marie's family arrived, through the Treaty of Turin (1860). In Marie's time, the city was undergoing rapid transformation: its population had risen from roughly 48,000 in 1861 to over 88,000 by 1872, driven by tourism, French administrative expansion, and Italian immigration. By the 1870s, Nice hosted an estimated 25,000 foreign winter residents annually, predominantly British but with substantial Russian, German, and other European colonies.

%% GLO_NICE.0003 %%
For Marie, Nice was simultaneously paradise and prison. She adored the Mediterranean beauty, the social spectacle of the Promenade, and the cosmopolitan winter season -- but despised the provincial limitations, the claustrophobic Russian colony, and the suffocating summer emptiness. Her eventual departure for Paris in 1877 to study art was experienced as liberation, though the family maintained their Nice properties and returned periodically. Marie's last visit was in 1882.

## Historical Context

### Annexation and Its Aftermath (1860-1870s)

%% GLO_NICE.0004 %%
Nice became French on June 14, 1860, when Napoleon III received the County of Nice (and Savoy) from King Victor Emmanuel II of Sardinia, as compensation for French military assistance in the Second Italian War of Independence against Austria. A plebiscite on April 15-16, 1860, produced an 83% "yes" vote, though opponents had called for abstention and significant irregularities were documented. The annexation triggered the *Nicard exodus* -- roughly a quarter of the population (11,000 people) emigrated to Italy, particularly to Ventimiglia, Bordighera, and Ospedaletti.

%% GLO_NICE.0005 %%
The French government pursued an aggressive policy of Francization: Italian, which had been the official administrative and cultural language, was immediately abolished and replaced by French. Italian-language newspapers were suppressed (*La Voce di Nizza* closed in 1861, *Il Diritto di Nizza* in 1871). Street names were francized, as were some surnames (Bianchi became Leblanc, Del Ponte became Dupont). In 1871, during the first free elections after the Franco-Prussian War, pro-Italian lists won overwhelmingly (26,534 out of 29,428 votes), and Garibaldi -- born in Nice -- was elected deputy. The resulting "Nicois Vespers" (February 8-10, 1871) saw popular demonstrations suppressed by 10,000 French soldiers.

%% GLO_NICE.0006 %%
By the time Marie arrived in the early 1870s, this political turmoil had largely subsided, but its effects lingered. The city retained a strong Italian cultural character: the local Nissart/Nicard dialect (an Occitan variety) remained the common language of the native population, and Italian influence pervaded architecture, cuisine, and daily life. Marie occasionally noted these linguistic layers -- she mentions hearing people speak "en vieux patois" (in old dialect) at the port (May 5, 1873), and her painting teacher M. Bensa was part of Nice's Italian-speaking artistic community.

### Three Communities

%% GLO_NICE.0007 %%
Nice in the 1870s comprised three overlapping but distinct communities:

1. **The Niçois**: The native population, speaking Nissart dialect, predominantly working-class and Catholic. Marie had little contact with them and occasionally expressed contempt -- "vilains, sales, bêtes Niçois" (ugly, dirty, stupid Niçois), she wrote in May 1873. The construction workforce, largely Italian immigrants, was driving the city's physical expansion.

2. **The French administration**: Officials, professionals, and military who had arrived after 1860 to impose French governance. The Avenue de la Gare (now Avenue Jean Médecin), connecting the PLM railway station to the old town, became the principal artery of the new French city.

3. **The foreign colonies**: The British, Russian, German, and other northern European communities who wintered in Nice from November through April. These seasonal visitors constituted the social world Marie inhabited, and their departure each spring left Nice a "désert" in her words.

### The Winter Season

%% GLO_NICE.0008 %%
The "season" (*la saison*) ran roughly from November to April, when wealthy northern Europeans descended on Nice to escape harsh winters and, for many, to seek the mild Mediterranean climate as therapy for respiratory ailments including tuberculosis. The medical consensus of the era held that warm, dry air and sunshine could arrest or cure consumption. Nice's average January temperature of around 10°C (50°F), combined with abundant sunshine and proximity to the sea, made it one of Europe's most prescribed winter resorts.

%% GLO_NICE.0009 %%
Winter visitors in the 1870s numbered roughly 8,000-20,000 staying for extended periods (over one month), with many more passing through for shorter stays. They occupied grand hotels, rented villas along the Promenade, and participated in a highly structured social calendar: the daily promenade, outdoor concerts ("la musique"), races at the Var hippodrome, regattas, pigeon shoots, carnival, and an endless round of balls, matinees, and private receptions. The season's social events were reported in local papers like *Le Phare du Littoral* and the society publication *La Saison*.

%% GLO_NICE.0010 %%
When the season ended, the contrast was brutal. Marie described post-season Nice repeatedly as a "desert": "Nice est un désert, et je voudrais le quitter au plus vite" (April 23, 1873). Summer was worse -- "les étés à Nice me tuent" (summers in Nice are killing me), she wrote on June 10, 1873, in an entry where tears fell on the paper: "Passer les étés ici, c'est la mort ! C'est perdre la moitié de la vie !"

## The Promenade des Anglais

%% GLO_NICE.0011 %%
The [Promenade des Anglais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Promenade_des_Anglais) was the social heart of Nice and the stage on which much of Marie's early diary unfolds. Originally constructed in the 1820s as a modest seaside path funded by the English colony's Reverend Lewis Way and the Holy Trinity Anglican Church, it was initially called *Camin deï Anglès* (Path of the English) in Nissart. It was widened and renamed "Promenade" in 1854, extended to Magnan by 1856, and continued to be improved through the 1870s. By Marie's time, it was a broad carriage road with a pedestrian path alongside, lined with palms and grand villas, running along the Baie des Anges.

%% GLO_NICE.0012 %%
For Marie, "la promenade" was both a place and a daily ritual. The afternoon promenade -- walking, riding, or driving along the seafront between roughly 3:00 and 6:00 PM -- was the principal occasion for seeing and being seen. Marie's diary entries from Nice almost invariably note what she wore ("robe bleue," "vêtement vigogne"), how much monde (society) was present, and whether she spotted the men she was interested in. The phrase "à la promenade" or "à la musique" (at the outdoor concert) appears hundreds of times in the early carnets. The daily promenade ritual was the engine of the entire social machine: alliances were formed, rivalries played out, and romantic prospects assessed in the public theater of the Promenade.

## Marie's Residences in Nice

%% GLO_NICE.0013 %%
The Bashkirtseff family occupied several properties in Nice, moving progressively westward along the Promenade des Anglais:

- **Villa Baquis** (earlier residence): Located on the Promenade area. Marie mentions it by name from January 1873. The villa had a terrace overlooking the Promenade des Anglais, from which Marie could observe the carriage traffic -- including, crucially, the Duke of Hamilton's distinctive four-horse carriage going to and from Gioia's residence. RSR comments in carnet 004 note "Villa Baquis explicitly mentioned" and describe its garden with a fountain surrounded by trees.
- **51 Promenade des Anglais**: An early family address on the Promenade.
- **55 bis Promenade des Anglais**: Subsequently acquired; Marie sometimes refers to it simply as "numéro 55." In March 1874, after the devastating Lewin ball rejection, Marie and her mother discussed selling "55" and fleeing to Naples.
- **63 Promenade des Anglais** (Villa Acquaviva / Villa Aqua Viva): The final and most enduring family residence in Nice, adjacent to what is now the Centre Universitaire Méditerranéen. A commemorative plaque marks Marie's stay. Marie spent seven years here.
- **Villa Meynadier**: The family negotiated for this property in spring 1873, as purchasing a villa signaled permanent establishment in Nice's elite community.

%% GLO_NICE.0014 %%
The family settled in Nice around 1871, joining the established Russian colony. However, as relatively minor Ukrainian-Russian nobility -- and with the social handicap of the parents' separation (Marie's father Konstantin remained in Ukraine) -- they occupied an ambiguous position. The family traveled without a patriarch, led by the grandmother. Villa ownership on the Promenade distinguished serious participants in the social season from mere hotel guests, and the Bashkirtseffs' progressive acquisition of better addresses reflected their efforts to establish social credentials.

## Social Venues

### Entertainment and Society

%% GLO_NICE.0015 %%
- **[Cercle Masséna](../social/CERCLE_MASSENA.md)**: Nice's most prestigious social club, founded in 1860. Located between the Avenue de la Victoire and surrounding streets, it hosted Saturday matinees, annual balls (notably the Lundi-Gras ball during Carnival), and regular social gatherings. Marie and her mother made calculated appearances: on March 1, 1873, they entered, made one circuit of the room, created a "sensation," and left after two minutes. The Cercle was a key venue for the social calculations that dominate the early diary.
- **[Cercle de la Méditerranée](../social/CERCLE_DE_LA_MEDITERRANEE.md)**: Another elite social club where concerts and performances took place. On February 7, 1873, the Vicomtesse Vigier (the former opera singer Sophie Cruvelli) hosted a Bellini performance there; Marie noted "Tout Nice y était" (all of Nice was there).
- **[Rumpelmayer](./RUMPELMAYER.md)**: Fashionable confectionery and cafe where the Bashkirtseffs took chocolate and ice cream. After the 1873 Carnival illuminations, the family went to Rumpelmayer's with the Anitchkoffs for "chocolat" and "glaces."
- **[Opera de Nice](../theaters/OPERA_NICE.md)** / **[Théâtre Français](../social/FRENCH_THEATER_NICE.md)**: Marie attended both Italian opera and French theater regularly. The Opera hosted Italian works (Verdi, Donizetti, Bellini), while the Théâtre Français presented French plays.
- **Casino Municipal**: A social and entertainment center. The actual Casino Municipal building was constructed 1882-1884 on the covered Paillon river, so during Marie's early years the term "casino" referred to earlier entertainment establishments.
- **Jetée-Promenade**: A pier and casino inspired by London's Crystal Palace, approved in 1875 but not opened until 1883. This was after Marie's primary Nice period but represents the city's Belle Époque ambitions.

### Cultural and Religious Sites

%% GLO_NICE.0016 %%
- **Russian Orthodox Church, Rue Longchamp**: The first Russian Orthodox church in Western Europe, consecrated in 1859 at 6 rue Longchamp, in the center of Nice. Built under constraints imposed by the Sardinian government (Roman Catholicism was the state religion): the church had to be unobtrusive, blend with surrounding buildings, and no bells were allowed. Financed by Dowager Empress Alexandra Feodorovna (widow of Nicholas I), it was where Alexander II was proclaimed heir to the throne. The Bashkirtseff family attended regularly; Marie describes Holy Week services, funerals (Mme Savelieff's in May 1873, attended by two Russian Grand Dukes), and the social dynamics of the congregation. See [Russian Orthodox Cathedral, Nice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Orthodox_Cathedral,_Nice) for the later, larger cathedral (1903-1912) on Avenue Nicholas II.
- **Notre-Dame de Nice**: Catholic church on the Avenue de la Gare (now Avenue Jean Médecin), built by the Jesuit Père Lavigne through collections and sermons. Marie visited with the Countess de Mouzay in May 1873 and delivered a fierce anticlerical critique of Lavigne: "une canaille, un prêtre catholique, aristocrate, c'est tout dire."
- **Nice Cathedral**: The historic Catholic cathedral in the old town.

### Other Locations

%% GLO_NICE.0017 %%
- **Avenue de la Gare** (now Avenue Jean Médecin): Nice's main commercial thoroughfare, connecting the PLM railway station to the seafront. Marie mentions passing it repeatedly during carriage rides.
- **Rue de France**: Major street running parallel to the seafront behind the Promenade. Marie knew Gioia's residence was on or near this street -- she could track the Duke of Hamilton's carriage "allant vers Gioia."
- **Cours Saleya** ("le Cours"): Main boulevard in the old town, where Marie viewed Carnival illuminations.
- **Rue Saint-François-de-Paule**: Old town street where electric illuminations were displayed during Carnival.
- **Nice Port**: Where Marie spotted yachts, including one with a ducal flag that she desperately hoped belonged to the Duke of Hamilton (May 3, 1873).
- **Var Racetrack** ("courses du Var"): Near the Var river, site of horse races that were a major social event of the winter season. Marie attended on February 5, 1873, noting "J'étais sur la tribune avec le plus beau monde de Nice."
- **Carabacel** / **Cimiez**: Hillside neighborhoods. The Bashkirtseffs rode horses to Carabacel with the Howards. Cimiez became particularly associated with the Russian colony.

## The Russian Colony

%% GLO_NICE.0018 %%
Nice's Russian community was among the most substantial on the Riviera. Russian aristocrats had been wintering in Nice since the 1850s, following Empress Alexandra Feodorovna's pioneering visit in 1856. After the railway reached Nice in 1864, Tsar Alexander II visited by train, cementing the Russian-Riviera connection. The community concentrated in certain neighborhoods (Saint-Philippe, Piol, and later Cimiez), maintaining a self-contained social infrastructure: the Orthodox church on Rue Longchamp, a parish library (founded by Prince Pierre Viazemsky in 1860), Russian bathhouses and barbers, and social networks centered on the church and reception days.

%% GLO_NICE.0019 %%
For Marie, the Russian colony was both comfort and constraint. It provided a familiar linguistic and cultural milieu -- she attended Russian Orthodox services, socialized with Russian families (the Anitchkoffs, Pattons, Savelieffs, Beketoffs), and participated in Russian social rituals. But the colony was small enough that gossip traveled fast, and the Bashkirtseffs' ambiguous status (wealthy but separated parents, Ukrainian rather than Petersburg aristocracy, not invited to the grandest receptions) caused Marie significant distress. Her diary is punctuated by moments of social humiliation traceable to the colony's internal hierarchies: not receiving invitations, being excluded from events hosted by higher-ranking families, and the constant awareness that "la colonie" was watching and judging.

### International Society

%% GLO_NICE.0020 %%
Beyond the Russian colony, Nice attracted a cosmopolitan mix:

- **British community**: The oldest and largest foreign colony, responsible for the Promenade des Anglais itself. The Anglican church, English confectioneries (London House was Marie's favorite), and English-language publications (*Swiss Times*, which Marie read to track the Duke of Hamilton's movements across Europe) anchored this community. By 1874, the British numbered roughly 25,000 among Nice's foreign residents.
- **Italian community**: Despite (or because of) the annexation, Italian cultural influence remained strong. Italian immigrants formed the construction workforce driving Nice's expansion. The local Nissart dialect was closer to Italian than French. Italian opera dominated the cultural scene.
- **Aristocracy from across Europe**: Dukes, princes, and noble families from France, Germany, Scandinavia, and beyond. The Duke of Hamilton, Marie's early obsession, exemplified this category -- a Scottish duke who wintered in Nice for the racing, pigeon shooting, and social life.

## Nice Carnival

%% GLO_NICE.0021 %%
The [Carnival of Nice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnival_of_Nice) was the climax of the winter social season, typically falling in February. In the 1870s, it was being transformed from a local festival into a major tourist attraction, with the young men of "la colonie" (the foreign community) organizing elaborate floats ("chars"), costumed cavalcades, and masked balls. Marie's extended account of the 1873 Carnival (February 23 and 25) provides vivid documentation: decorated floats, participants in white dominos and iron masks, exchanges of flowers and confetti, Boreel in his bandit costume, Nice violets (*violettes de Nice*) adorning society ladies' carriages, and evening illuminations on the Cours and Rue Saint-François-de-Paule followed by chocolate at Rumpelmayer's.

## Climate and Health Tourism

%% GLO_NICE.0022 %%
Nice's raison d'être as a winter resort was its climate. The city sits in a natural amphitheater sheltered from northern winds by the Alps, enjoying mild winters (average January temperature around 10°C), abundant sunshine, and low rainfall. From the late 18th century, British physicians had recommended the Riviera for consumptive patients, and by the 1870s, "climatotherapy" -- the idea that warm, dry Mediterranean air could treat or arrest tuberculosis -- was established medical doctrine. The British Medical Journal routinely published correspondence from doctors reporting on Nice's favorable weather conditions.

%% GLO_NICE.0023 %%
This health tourism context is significant for the diary. Marie's own health deteriorated in Nice's summers -- she describes physical decline in vivid terms: "teint a bruni, maigrie, yeux enfoncés, sacs sous les yeux, lèvres pâles, voix enrouée; je suis laide, très laide" (complexion darkened, thinned, sunken eyes, bags under eyes, pale lips, hoarse voice; I am ugly, very ugly) on June 10, 1873. The irony is profound: Nice was a health resort, but the very climate that attracted winter invalids made summers almost unbearable for a temperament like Marie's, and the chronic laryngitis that would eventually be diagnosed as the tuberculosis that killed her was already manifesting.

## Urban Development in the 1870s

%% GLO_NICE.0024 %%
Nice in Marie's time was a city in rapid physical transformation:

- **Railway**: The PLM (Paris-Lyon-Méditerranée) railway line had reached Nice in 1864, making it accessible from Paris in roughly 18 hours. This transformed the demographics of winter tourism from a small aristocratic elite to a broader wealthy bourgeoisie.
- **Promenade des Anglais expansion**: Continued widening and extension of the seafront promenade, with division between pedestrian paths and carriage roads visible in 1870s photographs.
- **Grand hotels and villas**: Construction boom along the Promenade and in new neighborhoods, driven by winter tourism demand.
- **Avenue de la Gare**: The new central artery of the French city, connecting the station to the sea, lined with shops and the newly built Notre-Dame church.
- **Public gardens and spaces**: Development of landscaped areas including the Jardin Public where outdoor concerts were held.

## Marie's Relationship with Nice

### What She Valued

%% GLO_NICE.0025 %%
- The beauty of the Mediterranean landscape -- lyrical descriptions of the Villa Baquis garden, the sea, the light
- The social spectacle of the winter season: the promenade, the races, the opera, the carnival
- Proximity to Monaco and other Riviera attractions
- The freedom of the Promenade for observing society and being observed
- The mild winter climate (when it cooperated -- rain days were socially catastrophic)

### What Frustrated Her

%% GLO_NICE.0026 %%
- The provincial character compared to Paris: "à Nice avec qui? Il n'y a que de la *racaille*, des ruinés, des joueurs, personne de convenable" (in Nice, marry whom? There's nothing but riffraff, ruined men, gamblers, nobody suitable) -- April 25, 1873
- Social exclusions: not receiving invitations to key events, the family's ambiguous status
- Limited artistic opportunities: only local teachers like M. Bensa and Manotte (piano)
- The claustrophobic Russian colony where gossip traveled fast
- The devastating seasonal emptiness when fashionable visitors departed
- The killing summers: heat, boredom, physical deterioration

### Nice vs. Paris

%% GLO_NICE.0027 %%
For Marie, Nice increasingly represented limitation while Paris represented possibility. Nice offered "bals, courses, chevaux, tirs, pique-niques, société" (balls, races, horses, shoots, picnics, society) but not the artistic training, intellectual stimulation, or social scale that Paris promised. Her eventual move to Paris in 1877 to study at the Académie Julian was experienced as liberation from Nice's constraints. However, the family continued to return periodically, and their Nice properties remained the family's base. Marie herself sang the praises of her adoptive city in other moods: "Nice is my country; Nice made me grow; Nice gave me health and a beautiful complexion" (quoted by Lisa Gerard-Sharp).
