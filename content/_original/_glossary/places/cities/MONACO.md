# Monaco

## Overview

%% GLO_MONACO.0002 %%
The Principality of Monaco -- and specifically its district of Monte Carlo -- was one of the most frequently mentioned places in Marie Bashkirtseff's diary. Perched on the Mediterranean coast just 20 km east of Nice, Monaco was undergoing a dramatic transformation during Marie's lifetime: from a near-bankrupt microstate in the 1850s to one of Europe's most glamorous destinations by the 1870s, thanks to the Casino de Monte-Carlo and the entrepreneurial genius of [Francois Blanc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fran%C3%A7ois_Blanc). Marie uses "Monaco" and "Monte-Carlo" largely interchangeably in her diary, though technically Monte Carlo is a district of the principality, named in 1866 after [Prince Charles III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_III,_Prince_of_Monaco).

%% GLO_MONACO.0003 %%
For the Bashkirtseff family, Monaco was practically an extension of daily life in Nice. Maman and ma tante went there constantly to gamble ("sauf les heures passees a la roulette de Bade ou de Monaco," Marie writes of her aunt's only personal pleasures). Marie herself attended the pigeon shooting, accompanied family members to the casino terraces, observed high society, and occasionally placed bets at roulette. Monaco was where she hoped to encounter the Duke of Hamilton during the 1873 season, where the family's associate Yourkoff killed himself in 1876, and where European aristocracy paraded in its most concentrated form.

## Historical Context

### From Bankruptcy to Belle Epoque (1856-1889)

%% GLO_MONACO.0004 %%
When [Charles III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_III,_Prince_of_Monaco) (Charles Honore Grimaldi, 1818-1889) succeeded his father Florestan as Prince of Monaco in 1856, the principality was in dire straits. The towns of Menton and Roquebrune -- comprising roughly 80% of Monaco's territory and population -- had rebelled during the 1848 revolutions and refused to pay taxes. In 1861, France formally annexed these towns through a treaty that also guaranteed Monaco's independence. Charles III was left with a tiny sovereign territory, negligible revenue, and a desperate need for a new economic model.

%% GLO_MONACO.0005 %%
His mother Princess Caroline had hatched the solution: build a casino resort to exploit Monaco's status as the only place in Europe where public gambling was legal, most Continental nations having banned it under Christian moralist pressure. Early attempts failed spectacularly. The concessionaires Aubert and Langlois (from 1856), then Frossard de Lilbonne, then Pierre Auguste Daval, all proved incompetent. Monaco had no roads connecting it to Nice, no decent hotels, and almost no visitors. The casino was moved several times before settling on the barren plateau of Les Spelugues (The Caves) in 1858, where a permanent building was constructed by architect Gobineau de la Bretonnerie (completed 1863), along with the adjacent Hotel de Paris (1862).

### Francois Blanc: The Magician of Monte Carlo

%% GLO_MONACO.0006 %%
Everything changed in 1863 when Francois Blanc (1806-1877) agreed to take over. Blanc was a French entrepreneur who had already transformed the casino at Bad Homburg near Frankfurt into Europe's most fashionable gambling resort, earning him the nickname "The Magician of Homburg." He and his twin brother Louis, born to a provincial tax officer in Courthezon (Vaucluse), had risen from circus hands to stock speculators (earning a criminal conviction and public fascination in equal measure) to casino magnates. At Homburg, Blanc had introduced the single-zero roulette wheel in 1843, improving the player's odds and drawing clientele away from the double-zero tables of Parisian casinos.

%% GLO_MONACO.0007 %%
Blanc formed the [Societe des Bains de Mer et du Cercle des Etrangers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soci%C3%A9t%C3%A9_des_Bains_de_Mer_de_Monaco) (SBM) with capital of 15 million francs and received a 50-year concession (lasting until 1913). He renamed the Spelugues plateau "Monte Carlo" in Prince Charles's honor. His genius lay in understanding that gambling alone was not enough -- he built an entire world around it: luxury hotels, lush tropical gardens, a grand spa, concerts, and aristocratic sporting events. He invested heavily in infrastructure, sponsoring the road and railway connections that made Monaco accessible.

%% GLO_MONACO.0008 %%
The results were staggering. By 1869, casino revenues were so large that Prince Charles III abolished all direct taxation for Monegasque citizens -- a policy that continues to this day. By 1870, Monaco boasted 150,000 annual visitors, up from fewer than 200 in the 1850s. When Blanc died at Loeche-les-Bains on July 27, 1877, he left a fortune of 72 million francs. His son Camille Blanc (1847-1927) succeeded him as head of SBM. Marie's diary records social contact with "Blanc" in the 1873-1874 Nice period, likely a member of this dynasty (see BLANC.md); "Mlle Blanc" appears separately in the aristocracy glossary as a social figure of the period.

### The Casino de Monte-Carlo

%% GLO_MONACO.0009 %%
The [Casino de Monte-Carlo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_Casino) was transformed and expanded in 1878-79 by architects Jules Dutrou (1819-1885) and Charles Garnier, the designer of the Paris Opera (Palais Garnier). Blanc had financed Garnier's work: he had provided a loan of at least 4.9 million gold francs to the cash-strapped French Third Republic so that the Paris opera house, begun in 1861, could be completed (it opened in 1875). Garnier's contributions to the Monte Carlo complex included the Salle Garnier, a 524-seat concert hall and opera house inaugurated on January 25, 1879, with a performance by Sarah Bernhardt. The first opera performed there was Robert Planquette's *Le Chevalier Gaston* on February 8, 1879. In 1880-81, the casino was expanded again with the Trente-et-Quarante Gaming Room, also by Garnier.

%% GLO_MONACO.0010 %%
The casino offered roulette, trente-et-quarante (thirty and forty), and other games. Monegasque citizens were (and remain) forbidden from entering the gaming rooms -- a rule imposed by Princess Caroline on moral grounds. The dress code and entry requirements were strict, reinforcing the casino's exclusivity. Marie notes the social stratification even within the casino: certain gaming rooms and terraces were reserved for higher-status visitors.

### The Railway Connection

%% GLO_MONACO.0011 %%
The PLM (Paris-Lyon-Mediterranee) railway reached Monaco in 1868, with the original Gare de Monaco opening in La Condamine and a second station in Monte Carlo in 1869, strategically positioned immediately below the Casino Gardens. The journey from Nice took approximately 30-45 minutes by train, with five daily services initially. In 1872, the Marseille-Genoa line was completed when the French and Italian railways met at Ventimiglia, making Monaco accessible from across Europe. This railway was the primary means by which Marie's family traveled to Monaco -- she mentions "le train de huit heures" (the eight o'clock train) that carried visitors between Nice and Monaco.

## The Tir aux Pigeons (Pigeon Shooting)

%% GLO_MONACO.0012 %%
The Pigeon-Shooting Club of Monte Carlo, established in 1872 on the coast opposite the casino, was one of Monaco's premier social institutions and a recurring presence in Marie's diary. Francois Blanc invested approximately 300,000 francs in its construction. The club was considered the most beautiful of its kind in Europe, surpassing even the equivalent Parisian establishment.

%% GLO_MONACO.0013 %%
Pigeon shooting (tir aux pigeons) had originated in England in the late eighteenth century and spread across Europe as an elite sport. Live pigeons were released from traps at distances of 20-25 meters and shot in flight. Membership required social standing and weapons proficiency. The annual January competition in Monaco was regarded as an unofficial world championship, attracting 80 to 116 participants daily. The coveted Grand Prix from the Monte Carlo casino was 20,000 francs, awarded to any shooter who downed 12 pigeons. Competitions ran three times weekly from January through April, with the season overlapping precisely with the Riviera's fashionable winter period.

%% GLO_MONACO.0014 %%
For Marie, the pigeon shooting was less about the sport than about the social spectacle. It was a venue where she could observe the Duke of Hamilton, European aristocrats, and the fashionable world. Her very first mentions of Monaco in the diary are linked to the Tir: she cries when a death in the family circle forces her to miss the pigeon shooting on February 10, 1873, honestly admitting her tears are more about missing the Duke than mourning the deceased. On March 10, 1873, she spots posters for the "grand concours international de tir aux pigeons" and her heart leaps at the possibility Hamilton might participate. She debates the morality of the sport with Yourkoff in Geneva (May 24, 1873), defending it against his objections. In February 1874, she notes that the pigeon shooting takes place "chaque vendredi" (every Friday) and that Blanc hosts dinners for all the shooters.

%% GLO_MONACO.0015 %%
The club switched entirely to mechanical targets in 1960 and ceased to exist in 1972. In 1979, the Auditorium Rainier III was built on the site, topped with Victor Vasarely's mosaic "Hexa Grace."

## Gambling Culture and Its Dark Side

%% GLO_MONACO.0016 %%
The Bashkirtseff family's relationship with Monaco's gambling tables was intimate and complicated. Maman and ma tante were regular visitors; Marie's aunt in particular seems to have found genuine solace at the roulette table -- Marie describes it as the only time she lived "pour elle" (for herself). Marie's grandfather "enerve" (irritated) her by constantly talking about Monaco "en prenant un ton que je ne trouve pas convenable, concernant les pelerinages qu'y font maman et ma tante" (October 10, 1874). Family members and associates gambled regularly, sometimes losing significant sums.

%% GLO_MONACO.0017 %%
Marie herself gambled on at least one documented occasion, on October 19, 1873: "je m'assis pres de maman et je jetais cent sous a noir, il y eut une serie, j'ai gagne cinq louis en mettant un louis a chaque coup." She won five louis (100 francs) by betting on black during a series -- a modest flutter by Monaco standards, but notable given that she was only fifteen. She reports the experience matter-of-factly, more interested in Mr. Anitchkoff presenting her as "une personne serieuse" than in the gaming itself. Years later, she recounts how the Howard girls had spread gossip that she "n'attendais d'etre grande que pour aller jouer a la roulette et m'amuser a Monaco" (July 30, 1884) -- a reputation she found unfair.

%% GLO_MONACO.0018 %%
The darker side of Monaco's gambling culture touched the Bashkirtseff circle directly. By 1875, a growing wave of anti-Monte Carlo propaganda emerged from Nice, including vivid claims of an epidemic of gambling-induced suicides. The Anglican Bishop of Gibraltar in 1876 called Monte Carlo "a scandal not only to our religion, but also to the civilization of this nineteenth century." While historian Mark Braude (author of *Making Monte Carlo: A History of Speculation and Spectacle*) argues there is no evidence suicide rates were ever much higher in Monte Carlo than elsewhere, and many tales were exaggerated or invented, some were real. Casino management was accused of concealing deaths -- whisking corpses through hidden panels, plucking them from gardens with rakes, even mailing bodies out of Monaco. Internal casino correspondence suggests that at least some cover-up efforts occurred.

### The Yourkoff Suicide (c. April 1876)

%% GLO_MONACO.0019 %%
The most searing intersection of Monaco's gambling world and Marie's personal life was the suicide of Yourkoff, Mme Sapogenikoff's companion of fifteen years, who killed himself at Monaco around April 1876. Marie's account (carnet 059, April-May 1876) is harrowing. "Hier, Yourkoff s'est tue a Monaco," she records. The Monaco authorities sealed his body in the morgue and refused to admit he was there until the Russian consul arrived with Yourkoff's suicide letter, addressed to "Nina" (Mme Sapogenikoff), announcing his death. Marie Sapogenikoff (the daughter) took the eight o'clock train to Monaco, found him "tout froid et mort," knelt, wept, and kissed his hand twice "comme c'est l'usage."

%% GLO_MONACO.0020 %%
Marie attributes Yourkoff's death not to gambling losses but to social humiliation: "C'est la seule cause de sa mort. On lui disait en face les choses les plus offensantes, on lui a fait un scandale au cercle de la Mediterranee." Society's cruelty toward him as Mme Sapogenikoff's lover (a social position without legitimacy) drove him to despair. The vicious gossip that followed -- rumors that Yourkoff had transferred his affections from mother to daughter -- appalled Marie. She concludes with characteristic ambivalence: "c'etait une noble nature au fond... on est tente de pardonner." The incident exemplifies how Monaco's glamorous surface concealed genuine human tragedy, and how Marie's diary cuts through social mythology to record what actually happened.

## Significance in Marie's Life

### Social Observatory

%% GLO_MONACO.0021 %%
Monaco functioned as Marie's most concentrated social observatory. The casino terraces, pigeon shooting grounds, and Hotel de Paris were stages where European aristocracy performed its rituals of display. Marie catalogues who she sees, what they wear, who accompanies whom, and what it all means for the social hierarchy. She is simultaneously fascinated and frustrated -- Monaco represents a world of adult sophistication she aspires to but cannot fully enter, both because of her age (she was 14-15 during the most intensive Monaco period) and because of her family's ambiguous social position.

### The Duke of Hamilton Connection

%% GLO_MONACO.0022 %%
During 1873, Monaco is inextricable from Marie's obsessive interest in the Duke of Hamilton. The Tir aux Pigeons was one of the few venues where she could reliably observe him. Her February 6, 1873 entry describes traveling to Monaco specifically for the pigeon shooting, where she notes the Duke has been "mis hors concours" (excluded from competition). She records his yacht visible from the terraces, his English companions, the cut of his clothes. The pigeon shooting season structured her emotional calendar: its end meant the Duke's departure from the Riviera, and with it the collapse of her romantic fantasies until the following winter.

### Monaco as Metaphor

%% GLO_MONACO.0023 %%
Marie repeatedly uses Monaco as shorthand for risk, loss, and the gambling mentality she both criticizes and embodies. "Lorsqu'on perd celui qu'on aime ce n'est pas perdre cent francs a Monaco" (October 23, 1873) -- losing the one you love is not the same as losing a hundred francs at Monaco. The comparison reveals that for Marie, Monaco losses are trivial, emotional losses devastating. In later years, she uses "Monaco" more abstractly: "il est peut-etre plus humiliant d'etre detrone a Monaco qu'a Paris" (June 6, 1883) -- being displaced in someone's affections at Monaco is perhaps more humiliating than in Paris, because the Riviera social world is smaller and more transparent.

## Geography and Access

%% GLO_MONACO.0024 %%
The Principality of Monaco occupies roughly 2 square kilometers along the Mediterranean coast between Nice and the Italian border. In Marie's time, it comprised several distinct areas:

- **Monaco-Ville** (Le Rocher): The historic old town on a rocky promontory, site of the Prince's Palace, the cathedral (completed 1886), and government buildings.
- **La Condamine**: The port area below the rock, with the original railway station (1868).
- **Monte Carlo**: The district created by Blanc on the former Spelugues plateau, home to the casino, Hotel de Paris, Cafe de Paris, the Salle Garnier, and the pigeon shooting grounds. A second railway station opened here in 1869, directly below the Casino Gardens.

%% GLO_MONACO.0025 %%
From Nice, the journey to Monaco followed the spectacular Corniche coast. By train (PLM railway, from 1868), the trip took 30-45 minutes. By carriage along the coastal roads, it was slower but scenic. The family typically took the train -- Marie mentions the "train de huit heures" and returning in the evening. The ease of access made Monaco a casual day trip rather than a major expedition, which is why it appears so frequently in daily life.

## Cultural Institutions

### The Salle Garnier (Opera de Monte-Carlo)

%% GLO_MONACO.0026 %%
The [Salle Garnier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Op%C3%A9ra_de_Monte-Carlo), designed by Charles Garnier and inaugurated January 25, 1879, added a cultural dimension to Monte Carlo's entertainment offerings. Construction took only 8 months and 16 days. The 524-seat hall (compared to 2,000 at the Palais Garnier) was intimate and lavishly decorated. Initially a concert hall, it was soon used for opera and remodeled in 1898-99 by Henri Schmit to improve the stage area. By the early twentieth century it would host legendary performers including Nellie Melba, Enrico Caruso, and Feodor Chaliapin.

### Hotel de Paris

%% GLO_MONACO.0027 %%
The Hotel de Paris Monte-Carlo, designed by Gobineau de la Bretonnerie and constructed in 1862, was the premier accommodation for casino visitors. Its restaurant and terraces were important social venues. Marie mentions dining there and observing society from the terraces.

Heaviest concentrations in carnets 002-003 (February-April, pigeon shooting season), 007 (July-August, summer excursions), and 010-011 (September-November, return to Nice).

%% GLO_MONACO.0029 %%
**1874** (Carnets 015-027): Continued frequent references. Marie reads "l'Histoire de Monaco" (April 15, 1875, carnet 031). Pigeon shooting continues as social anchor. Family visitors stay at Monaco and commute to Nice. Georges gets drunk and slaps Prodgers at Monte Carlo (October 19, 1874).

%% GLO_MONACO.0030 %%

Monaco references in carnets 071-072 (1877) are particularly dense, suggesting renewed engagement during the last Nice season before Marie's move to Paris.

%% GLO_MONACO.0031 %%
**1878-1884** (Carnets 077-105): Monaco appears less frequently as Marie's life centers on Paris and her art career. Mentions become more reflective and metaphorical. The aunt's gambling habit is noted (1883). Marie's reputation as a Monaco-obsessed girl is bitterly recalled (1884).
