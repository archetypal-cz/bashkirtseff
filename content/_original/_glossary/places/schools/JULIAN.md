# Académie Julian

%% GLO_JULIAN.0002 %%
**Research Status**: Comprehensive
**Last Updated**: 2026-05-24
**Diary Coverage**: 1877-1884 (carnets 073-106)
**References**: ~197 direct references as "Julian"; many hundreds more as "l'atelier"

%% GLO_JULIAN.0003 %%
## Overview

%% GLO_JULIAN.0004 %%
The Académie Julian was a private art school in Paris, founded in 1868 by [Rodolphe Julian](../../people/mentioned/RODOLPHE_JULIAN.md) (1839-1907). It was the single most important institution in Marie Bashkirtseff's life after 1877 -- her workplace, her community, her proving ground, and by her own account her refuge: "Heureusement il y a l'atelier. Avec l'atelier je n'ai peur de rien" -- "Fortunately there is the atelier. With the atelier I fear nothing" (January 23, 1878).

%% GLO_JULIAN.0005 %%
Unlike the state-run [École des Beaux-Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89cole_des_Beaux-Arts), which excluded women entirely until 1897 and required a French-language entrance examination that deterred many foreigners, the Académie Julian admitted anyone who could pay the fees. This made it the most important venue for serious art training for two groups: women and foreign students. By 1885 the women's section alone had 400 students; by 1889, 600, drawn from over 50 nationalities.

%% GLO_JULIAN.0006 %%
## Rodolphe Julian: The Founder

%% GLO_JULIAN.0007 %%
Pierre Louis Rodolphe Julian was born on June 13, 1839, in [Lapalud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lapalud), a small town in the Vaucluse department of southeastern France. He came from modest origins, worked as a bookshop employee in Marseille, and was notably interested in sports, particularly wrestling -- a pastime associated with his native Lapalud. George Moore described him as having a physique like "a kind of Hercules" -- dark-haired, broad-shouldered, with short legs, "a soft voice and all the charm of the Midi."

%% GLO_JULIAN.0008 %%
Julian moved to Paris and studied painting under [Léon Cogniet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%A9on_Cogniet) and [Alexandre Cabanel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandre_Cabanel) without formal enrollment at the École des Beaux-Arts. He first exhibited at the Salon in 1863 but achieved only moderate success as a painter. His true vocation proved to be as an art educator and impresario. In 1868 he founded his academy in a small room in the [Passage des Panoramas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passage_des_Panoramas), one of the oldest covered shopping arcades in Paris, located in the 2nd arrondissement between Boulevard Montmartre and Rue Saint-Marc.

%% GLO_JULIAN.0009 %%
Julian received the [Légion d'honneur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legion_of_Honour) in 1881 for his services to the arts. In 1895 he married [Amélie Beaury-Saurel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Am%C3%A9lie_Beaury-Saurel), a painter who had been his student and then the *massière* (elected student manager) of the women's atelier at the Passage des Panoramas. Julian died on February 2, 1907.

%% GLO_JULIAN.0010 %%
Marie called him "Julian" or "le père Julian" and described him as supportive but commercially astute. He recognized her talent early and warned her that her rapid progress was creating jealousy: "Quand on arrive à donner des inquiétudes à celles qui avaient l'habitude d'être premières... on est exposé à mille petites choses quelquefois désagréables" -- "When one begins to worry those who were accustomed to being first... one is exposed to a thousand small things that are sometimes unpleasant" (January 2, 1878). When a model arrived drunk and failed to pose, Julian improvised; when the atelier needed publicity for the Salon, he shrewdly commissioned paintings of the studio itself (see *L'Académie Julian* below).

%% GLO_JULIAN.0011 %%
## Locations

%% GLO_JULIAN.0012 %%
The Académie Julian operated multiple ateliers across Paris. At its peak it had approximately 17 locations, 7 of which were devoted to women. The principal addresses were:

%% GLO_JULIAN.0013 %%
- **27, galerie Montmartre, Passage des Panoramas** (2nd arr.) -- The original location, opened 1868. This was the women's atelier where Marie studied from 1877. The Passage des Panoramas was a covered arcade dating from 1800, the oldest in Paris, known for its glazed roof and gas lighting (installed 1817). The academy occupied space at the upper level of the passage.
- **51, rue Vivienne** (2nd arr.) -- A second women's atelier opened in November 1880 under the supervision of Amélie Beaury-Saurel. Marie's diary records the split: 22 students transferred to the new location from her original atelier.
- **5, rue de Berri** (8th arr.) -- A major atelier near the Champs-Élysées. This became the longest-running location after the Passage des Panoramas.
- **31, rue du Dragon** (6th arr.) -- An atelier in the Left Bank. The building still stands.

## How the Academy Worked

%% GLO_JULIAN.0015 %%
### Admission and Fees

%% GLO_JULIAN.0016 %%
There was no entrance exam and no age limit. Anyone could enroll by paying the fees. Women paid approximately double what men paid, reflecting both the higher costs of maintaining separate studios and the captive market -- women had so few alternatives for serious training. Based on 1902 fee records:
- **Women**: 60 francs/month part-time, 100 francs/month full-time; 400 francs/year part-time, 700 francs/year full-time
- **Men**: 25 francs/month part-time, 50 francs/month full-time; 200 francs/year part-time, 400 francs/year full-time

(In the 1870s-80s when Marie attended, fees were somewhat lower but the gender disparity was comparable -- approximately 500 francs/year for women versus 300 for men.)

%% GLO_JULIAN.0017 %%
### Daily Schedule

%% GLO_JULIAN.0018 %%
The school operated from approximately 8:00 AM to 6:00 PM or later. Students worked from live models, who were paid for eight hours daily year-round -- unlike competing academies that closed for the summer. This year-round availability was a major selling point. Models were paid from a common fund (the *masse*) collected by the *massier* or *massière*.

%% GLO_JULIAN.0019 %%
Each Monday, places in the atelier were available on a first-come-first-served basis, and each student kept her spot for the rest of the week. This led to fierce territorial disputes -- Marie nearly quarreled with Breslau when the latter "with very ugly indelicacy had taken my place on a wretched pretext" during her absence (January 8, 1878). The best spots, closest to the model with the best light and angle, were jealously guarded.

%% GLO_JULIAN.0020 %%
### The Massier/Massière System

%% GLO_JULIAN.0021 %%
The *massier* (male) or *massière* (female) was a student elected by peers to manage the common affairs of the atelier. The role originated in the French art academy tradition and involved:
- Collecting monthly contributions (the *masse*) from each student
- Paying for models, heating, lighting, and supplies
- Organizing model selection -- the *massière* took a vote of the students to select models each week
- Maintaining the studio -- cleaning, arranging easels, managing the stove
- Serving as liaison between students and the patron (head professor) or director

%% GLO_JULIAN.0022 %%
At the Académie Julian, Amélie Beaury-Saurel served as *massière* of the Passage des Panoramas women's atelier beginning in the 1880s, progressing from administrative duties to model selection and eventually to teaching. She married Julian in 1895 and continued supervising the women's ateliers.

%% GLO_JULIAN.0023 %%
### Teaching Method

%% GLO_JULIAN.0024 %%
The academy followed the pedagogical principles of the École des Beaux-Arts while emphasizing freedom of individual expression. The core curriculum was:
- **Drawing from casts**: Beginners copied plaster casts of antique sculptures
- **Drawing from the live model**: Students drew and painted from nude models (male nudes were used in the women's classes -- revolutionary for the time, and a privilege not available at state institutions)
- **Painting from the model**: Advanced students worked in oils
- **Composition**: Students developed their own compositions for Salon submission

%% GLO_JULIAN.0025 %%
Two weekly *correction* sessions were held by visiting professors -- distinguished Academic painters who examined each student's work, offered critique, and occasionally demonstrated technique. The key instructors at the women's atelier during Marie's years were:

%% GLO_JULIAN.0026 %%
- **[Tony Robert-Fleury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tony_Robert-Fleury)** (1837-1911): The principal visiting professor and the teacher whose opinion Marie valued most. Son of the painter Joseph-Nicolas Robert-Fleury, trained under Delaroche and Cogniet. Known for historical paintings (*Le Dernier Jour de Corinthe*, *Le Dr Pinel à la Salpêtrière*). Marie called him simply "Tony." His early encouragement was decisive: in December 1877 he spent thirty minutes examining Marie's drawings, asked whether she had ever painted, and declared she could begin painting whenever she wished. He later became president of the Société des Artistes Français and sat on the Salon jury -- making him a crucial advocate for his students' careers. See [#Robert_Fleury](../../people/artists/ROBERT_FLEURY.md).

%% GLO_JULIAN.0027 %%
- **[Jules Joseph Lefebvre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jules_Joseph_Lefebvre)** (1836-1911): The second regular visiting professor. Winner of the Prix de Rome (1861), member of the Académie des Beaux-Arts (1891), president of the jury at the Société des Artistes Français. Known for idealized female nudes (*La Vérité*, *Diana Surprised*, *Lady Godiva*). Characterized as "an excellent and sympathetic teacher" who taught over 1,500 students. Less frequently mentioned in Marie's diary than Robert-Fleury, but his jury position made him influential for Salon acceptance.

%% GLO_JULIAN.0028 %%
- **Other notable professors** across the academy's various ateliers: [William-Adolphe Bouguereau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William-Adolphe_Bouguereau), [Jean-Paul Laurens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Paul_Laurens), Gabriel Ferrier, [Gustave Boulanger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustave_Boulanger), Marcel Baschet, Henri Royer, and the former student-turned-instructor [Edgar Chahine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edgar_Chahine).

%% GLO_JULIAN.0029 %%
The strategy of hiring professors who simultaneously served on the Salon jury and the École des Beaux-Arts admission panels was commercially brilliant. Julian's students had a systematic advantage: in 1904, 44 of 107 male students accepted at the École des Beaux-Arts came from the Académie Julian, and among women, 7 of 10 acceptances were Julian alumni. An 1889 petition in *La Curiosité Universelle* exposed this favoritism, noting that Jules Lefebvre "admitted without ceremony four of five" of his own students to the École.

%% GLO_JULIAN.0030 %%
Students who preferred independence could conceal their work during the professor's visit -- the *Atelier Libre* tradition offered creative autonomy alongside the structured training.

%% GLO_JULIAN.0031 %%
### The Concours System

%% GLO_JULIAN.0032 %%
Julian organized regular competitions (*concours*) among his students, judged by the visiting professors. These were the primary measure of progress and the source of Marie's most intense anxieties and triumphs:

%% GLO_JULIAN.0033 %%
- **Frequency**: Monthly (sometimes described as fortnightly in different periods). A concours typically ran for one week: students worked from a set model or subject, then the visiting professor ranked the submissions.
- **Format**: All students in an atelier worked from the same model in the same pose. The model was selected at the start of the week. Subjects were sometimes specified (e.g., a head study, a figure study, a clothed figure with hands).
- **Judging**: The visiting professors ranked students' work in order of merit. Medals and small prizes of 100 francs were awarded to the best work.
- **Inter-atelier competitions**: Periodically, the concours was held across all ateliers -- four or more studios of both sexes working the same subject, their work judged together. Marie's dream was to beat the men's ateliers: "Si j'ai une mention cette année j'aurai marché plus vite que Breslau" (April 18, 1883). "Battre deux ateliers d'hommes, deux cents élèves. Mais c'est impossible..." -- "To beat two men's ateliers, two hundred students. But it is impossible..." (April 18, 1883).

%% GLO_JULIAN.0034 %%
Marie tracked her concours rankings obsessively. The night before results she wrote: "Le concours sera jugé demain... J'ai si peur d'être mal placée" -- "The competition will be judged tomorrow... I am so afraid of being poorly placed" (January 29, 1878). After a disappointing result she raged: "Si je n'ai pas monté c'est la faute de Cassagnac. Je lui paierai ça avec sa colonelle!" -- "If I have not advanced it is Cassagnac's fault. I will make him pay for it with his colonelle!" (January 29, 1878). She recognized that sleeping only three hours a night during the competition week -- consumed by the Cassagnac social whirl -- was the real cause: "Croyez-vous que si je dormais plus de trois heures par nuit la semaine du concours, je serais placée comme je l'ai été?" -- "Do you think that if I slept more than three hours a night during concours week, I would have placed as I did?" (January 31, 1878).

%% GLO_JULIAN.0035 %%
Tony Robert-Fleury declared that Marie's competition head study was "ce que j'ai fait de mieux jusqu'à présent" -- "the best thing I have done so far" (December 11, 1880). In one inter-atelier concours in 1883, Amélie Beaury-Saurel was judged first, then five men, then Marie, then Filippini (May 31, 1883). Marie's response: "Les concours de tout temps ont causé bien des stupéfactions" -- "Concours have always caused plenty of astonishment" (May 31, 1883).

%% GLO_JULIAN.0036 %%
## The Women's Atelier

%% GLO_JULIAN.0037 %%
The women's atelier was physically separate from the men's ateliers but studied an identical curriculum. Women worked from the same live models -- including nude male models -- in the same poses, a radical departure from the conventions of the time. At state institutions and most private academies, women were restricted to draped figures, plaster casts, and still lifes.

%% GLO_JULIAN.0038 %%
The women's section grew explosively: from approximately 40 students at its formal establishment in 1880, to 400 by 1885, to 600 by 1889. The student body was remarkably international -- Americans, Russians, Poles, Scandinavians, Brazilians, British, Hungarians, and others worked side by side. Marie refers to her fellow students by nationality: "les Suissesses" (the Swiss women), "la Polonaise" (the Polish woman), the "femme hongroise," and so on.

%% GLO_JULIAN.0039 %%
The atmosphere was competitive, intimate, and sometimes vicious. Marie's diary captures the social dynamics with novelistic precision: alliances forming and breaking, territorial disputes over easel positions, gossip and jealousy, and the intense bond between the handful of genuinely talented students. She was contemptuous of most of her classmates: "Tout cela c'est de la canaille et... incapable" -- "All of them are rabble and... incapable" (December 28, 1877). Only Breslau met her standards: "Il n'y a que nous deux" -- "There are only the two of us" (December 28, 1877).

%% GLO_JULIAN.0040 %%
At the same time, the atelier was a place of warmth and camaraderie. The students held New Year's parties where they told fortunes: "Breslau aura des couronnes, moi le prix de Rome" -- "Breslau will have crowns, and I the Prix de Rome" (December 31, 1877). Marie sent humorous telegrams to absent friends, playfully comparing the women's atelier to an "écurie de femmes" -- a "stable of women" (December 24, 1877). She acknowledged that "l'atelier seul me sauve" -- "the atelier alone saves me" (December 25, 1877).

%% GLO_JULIAN.0041 %%
The nude model remained a source of social tension. When Marie's acquaintance Berthe Boyd visited the atelier, Marie fretted: "Le modèle nu; j'ai prévenu et beaucoup parlé des bains de mer et du caleçon" -- "The nude model; I warned her and spoke a great deal about bathing suits" (January 1, 1878). When Julian himself entered while a nude woman was posing, Berthe fled the room (January 3, 1878).

%% GLO_JULIAN.0042 %%
## Relationship with the École des Beaux-Arts

%% GLO_JULIAN.0043 %%
The Académie Julian functioned simultaneously as an alternative to and a preparatory school for the [École des Beaux-Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89cole_des_Beaux-Arts), the prestigious state-run academy:

%% GLO_JULIAN.0044 %%
- **Women's exclusion**: The École des Beaux-Arts did not admit women until 1897 -- thirteen years after Marie's death. Even after that date, access remained restricted: only one same-sex workshop was open to women until the end of the 1920s, and women could not compete for the Prix de Rome until 1903.
- **Foreign students**: The École required a French-language entrance exam that deterred many international students. Julian had no language requirement.
- **Entrance competition**: The École demanded a rigorous multi-day entrance competition: 12 hours of life drawing, 2 hours of anatomy drawing, 4 hours of perspective, 6 hours of architecture exercises, and oral/written exams on art history. Julian simply required fees.
- **Favored pipeline**: Julian's hiring of École professors as visiting instructors created a direct pipeline. His students were systematically favored in admissions, creating what amounted to a preparatory school for the state institution.

%% GLO_JULIAN.0045 %%
Marie was acutely aware of this institutional landscape. She visited the École des Beaux-Arts to see the Prix de Rome concours (July 23, 1879; July 25, 1883) and was told by Émile Bastien-Lepage that "avec un ensemble comme mon dernier je serais reçue au concours de place à l'École" -- "with a submission like my last I would pass the entrance competition for the École" (July 28, 1883). The Prix de Rome winner Marcel Baschet was from "chez Julian" (July 28, 1883) -- proof of the academy's competitive power.

%% GLO_JULIAN.0046 %%
## Marie's Experience (1877-1884)

%% GLO_JULIAN.0047 %%
### Enrollment and Early Years (1877-1879)

%% GLO_JULIAN.0048 %%
Marie enrolled at the Académie Julian in October 1877, shortly before turning 19, after tuberculosis had destroyed her singing voice and ended her hopes for a career in opera. The transition from aspiring diva to serious visual artist was swift and total. From her first weeks she threw herself into studio work with an intensity that startled both teachers and fellow students. Julian noticed immediately: within two months he confided that Marie's rapid progress was creating jealousy among other students (January 2, 1878).

%% GLO_JULIAN.0049 %%
The daily routine was grueling. Marie rose early, worked from the live model through the day, and returned home exhausted. She took anatomy lessons with the Swiss students and drew after hours. Julian told her to draw feet "dans la perfection" -- holding her to the highest standard (January 8, 1878). Robert-Fleury's first extended examination of her work (December 29, 1877) -- thirty minutes of careful study, followed by the question whether she had ever painted -- was the decisive validation that launched her career.

%% GLO_JULIAN.0050 %%
### The Mature Period (1880-1882)

%% GLO_JULIAN.0051 %%
By 1880, Marie was one of the leading students. Tony Robert-Fleury's praise intensified: "Nous avons l'air de faire un concours avec Breslau et je l'ai battue" -- "We seem to be doing a competition with Breslau and I beat her" (May 8, 1880). In December 1880, Julian commissioned both Marie and Amélie to paint the atelier itself for the Salon: Marie was to paint the Passage des Panoramas women's atelier with life-size figures; Amélie was to paint the rue Vivienne atelier in smaller scale. The scene of the commission is vivid: Julian made both women sign a written engagement, "half laughing, half serious," with witnesses, then demonstrated the advantages of the subject for a full half hour (December 24, 1880). Amélie "burst into tears immediately" when Julian emphasized Marie's superior drawing skills.

%% GLO_JULIAN.0052 %%
The resulting painting, *L'Académie Julian* (also known as *In the Studio*, 1881), is one of Marie's finest works. The oil on canvas (154 x 186 cm) depicts the women's atelier in full activity: students working from a model on a raised platform, the studio bathed in light from tall windows. Julian's commercial instinct was sound -- the painting served as advertisement for his school while also being a significant artistic achievement. It was exhibited at the Salon of 1881 and now resides in the Dnipro State Art Museum (Ukraine).

%% GLO_JULIAN.0053 %%
### The Final Years (1882-1884)

%% GLO_JULIAN.0054 %%
By 1882-1883, Marie had largely outgrown student exercises. She maintained her connection to the Académie Julian but increasingly worked in her own studio, painting portraits, Salon submissions, and ambitious multi-figure compositions. She still participated in inter-atelier concours and valued the competition: "Savez-vous ce que je fais? Je fais un concours chez Julian" -- "Do you know what I am doing? I am entering a concours at Julian's" (April 18, 1883). She dreamed of beating the men's ateliers -- 200 students -- knowing it was "impossible" but "grateful for any recognition."

%% GLO_JULIAN.0055 %%
Even as tuberculosis advanced, art remained her anchor. She continued attending the atelier intermittently through 1884. Her last diary entries reference the studio and her ongoing work. The Académie Julian was the constant in a life that was running out of time.

%% GLO_JULIAN.0056 %%
## Key People at the Academy

%% GLO_JULIAN.0057 %%
### Marie's Fellow Students

%% GLO_JULIAN.0058 %%
- **[Louise Catherine Breslau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louise_Catherine_Breslau)** (1856-1927): Marie's chief rival. German-born, Swiss-raised, four years older. Consistently placed first where Marie placed second. Marie acknowledged her talent with grudging admiration -- "her work has nothing feminine, banal, or misshapen" (November 22, 1878) -- while resenting her success. Breslau went on to become the first foreign woman artist to receive the Légion d'honneur. See [#Breslau](../../people/artists/BRESLAU_LOUISE.md).
- **[Amélie Beaury-Saurel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Am%C3%A9lie_Beaury-Saurel)** (1848-1924): Fellow student who became *massière*, then instructor, then Julian's wife (1895). Won the first prize in an inter-atelier concours in 1883 (beating Marie). Marie noted her jealousy: "Amélie me mangerait" -- "Amélie would eat me alive" (January 2, 1878).
- **[Anna Bilińska-Bohdanowicz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anna_Bili%C5%84ska-Bohdanowicz)** (1857-1893): Polish painter, another notable student from the same period.
- **"Les Suissesses"**: Marie's name for the Swiss students who formed her closest circle: Breslau, [Sophie Schaeppi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophie_Schaeppi), Zeller, and Forchhammer (nicknamed "l'agneau" -- "the lamb" -- for her meekness).
- **Filippini**: Student who placed just after Marie in the 1883 inter-atelier concours.
- **Mlle de Villevielle**: A "very well-born" student from the Faubourg Saint-Germain aristocracy.

%% GLO_JULIAN.0062 %%
## Historical Significance

%% GLO_JULIAN.0063 %%
The Académie Julian played a crucial role in the democratization of art education in late 19th-century France. Its significance rests on several factors:

%% GLO_JULIAN.0064 %%
1. **Women's access to professional training**: Before 1897, the Académie Julian was the primary venue where women could receive the same level of training as men -- including life drawing from nude models, a skill considered indispensable for serious art but denied to women at state institutions as "indecent."
2. **International art education**: Students from over 50 nationalities attended. The school was particularly important for American, Canadian, Scandinavian, Brazilian, and Eastern European artists who could not easily access the French state system.
3. **Salon preparation**: Julian's school produced numerous Salon exhibitors and medal winners, including Prix de Rome recipients, making it the most effective pathway from private training to public recognition.
4. **Art-historical legacy**: The school trained artists who participated in nearly every major movement from Academic painting through Post-Impressionism, Fauvism, and Cubism.

%% GLO_JULIAN.0065 %%
The Académie Julian operated continuously from 1868 until 1968, when it was integrated into ESAG Penninghen, a design school that still occupies the premises at 31 rue du Dragon. During World War II, the school was forced to close temporarily under Nazi "degenerate art" policies.
