## Overview

%% GLO_FANNY_LEAR.0002 %%
%% 2026-06-06T10:00:00 RSR: Researched via WebSearch (Wikipedia, Michael R. Thompson Rare Books, 19thcenturyphotos.com, theromanovfamily.com) and corroborated with diary text in carnets 050, 054, 055, 056, 057, 058, 059, 064, 072. %%
"Fanny Lear" was the literary pseudonym of Harriet Clarissima Ely Blackford (c. 1848–1886), an American adventuress and courtesan born in Philadelphia, Pennsylvania. She is notorious as the mistress of Grand Duke Nikolai Konstantinovich of Russia (1850–1918), nephew of Tsar Alexander II, and as the central figure in the 1874 St. Petersburg diamond-theft scandal. She published a candid memoir about her Russian adventures under her pen name.

## The Scandal (1874)

%% GLO_FANNY_LEAR.0006 %%
%% 2026-06-06T10:00:00 RSR: The diamond scandal is well documented. The icon in question belonged to Grand Duchess Alexandra Iosifovna (mother of Grand Duke Nikolai Konstantinovich), kept in the Marble Palace, St. Petersburg. The diamonds had been set into the icon by Emperor Nicholas I as a wedding gift. The Grand Duke's motive was to pay debts from lavish gifts to Fanny Lear. %%
Grand Duke Nikolai Konstantinovich stole three large diamonds from the setting of a precious family icon — the icon that Emperor Nicholas I had given to bless the marriage of Grand Duke Konstantin Nikolaevich and Grand Duchess Alexandra Iosifovna — to pay debts incurred buying gifts for Fanny Lear. The theft was discovered in April 1874. To avoid a public trial, on 13 December 1874 the Grand Duke was formally declared insane by imperial decree — the only Romanov dynast ever so declared — and placed under guardianship. Fanny Lear was arrested and immediately expelled from Russia by the police. The Grand Duke was exiled, first to Orenburg and later to Tashkent.

## Memoir

%% GLO_FANNY_LEAR.0008 %%
%% 2026-06-06T10:00:00 RSR: Full bibliographic data from Michael R. Thompson Rare Books listing: Harriet Clarissima Ely Blackford, *Le Roman d'une Américaine en Russie, accompagné de lettres originales* (Brussels: Lacroix et Cie, 1875). Blackford reportedly wrote the account in English over eleven days, then supervised its French translation. %%
Expelled from Russia, Fanny Lear wrote her memoir in English over approximately eleven days, then supervised its translation into French. It was published in Brussels as *Le Roman d'une Américaine en Russie, accompagné de lettres originales* (Lacroix et Cie, 1875). The book was an instant bestseller and a significant scandal — it included original letters from the Grand Duke, some containing politically sensitive material about Russian intentions toward British India. Marie Bashkirtseff and her family obtained a copy in December 1875 and Marie read passages aloud at table (carnet 050, 1875-12-05).

## The Italy Connection (1876)

%% GLO_FANNY_LEAR.0010 %%
%% 2026-06-06T10:00:00 RSR: The Mirafiori mentioned in the diary is Emanuele Alberto Guerrieri, Conte di Mirafiori e Fontanafredda (1851–1894), the morganatic son of King Vittorio Emanuele II of Italy and Rosa Vercellana (La Bela Rosin). He was brother-in-law to the Comte de Larderei (his wife was a Larderei). The Italian expulsion is documented in carnets 056 (1876-03-31) and 056 (1876-04-02). %%
After her expulsion from Russia, Fanny Lear traveled widely in Europe and was herself a celebrity of scandal. By early 1876 she was in Italy, where she became entangled with the Conte di Mirafiori (morganatic son of King Vittorio Emanuele II of Italy), who was married to a sister of the Comte de Larderei. This involvement was considered sufficiently compromising that the King ordered her expulsion from Italy: she was summoned to the Ministry, politely informed she must leave, and had her hotel bill and travel expenses paid to the border. According to Marie's diary (carnet 056, 1876-03-31), Fanny Lear replied to the minister's announcement simply: "Comme vous êtes bête!" — turned her back, and walked out. Marie herself encountered Fanny Lear in Rome in March 1876 (carnet 055, 1876-03-24) and described her as "une jolie femme, mais une expression malheureuse."

## Name Note

%% GLO_FANNY_LEAR.0015 %%
%% 2026-06-06T10:00:00 RSR: The Grand Duke confusion is easy to make: there were multiple Grand Dukes named Nikolai in the Romanov family. The relevant one here is Nikolai KONSTANTINOVICH (son of Grand Duke Konstantin Nikolaevich), not Nikolai Alexandrovich (who was Alexander II's son and died in 1865). All references in the diary (carnet 050: "grand-duc Nicolas Constantinovitch"; carnet 054/056/064 passim) point unambiguously to Nikolai Konstantinovich. The LAN note in carnet 050 and RSR note in carnet 054 correctly identify him. %%
The pseudonym "Fanny Lear" plays on the name of Shakespeare's King Lear, perhaps evoking exile and tragic reversal. In French sources and in Marie's diary, "Fanny Lear" is used as if it were a real name; Harriet Blackford's birth name never appears in Marie's text. The Grand Duke involved was Nikolai Konstantinovich (1850–1918), son of Grand Duke Konstantin Nikolaevich — not to be confused with Nikolai Alexandrovich, Tsarevich (1843–1865, died before the scandal).
